# PocketBase qualitative comparison

PocketBase made the first server leg unusually tangible. Downloading one small binary, binding it locally, and creating a superuser gave an immediately inspectable backend without account setup or cloud control-plane work. For a prototype like Clique, that compactness is appealing: the database and administration surface are physically in the lane, and the REST/realtime model has a pleasantly direct shape. The `serve --help` output also made the local network setup legible rather than magical.

This attempt did not reach the point where PocketBase could demonstrate a full social messenger implementation. The local app shell used a deliberately small application API, so I cannot honestly turn that into a claim about its collection rules, realtime subscriptions, file protections, migrations, or scheduled hooks under the benchmark's difficult behaviors. In particular, offline convergence, server-enforced ephemeral media, session invalidation, and the scale replay all demand more than a CRUD-shaped proof. The absence of browser choreography also means the nice early bootstrapping experience must be kept separate from product validation.

Compared with Snapback, PocketBase feels more self-contained and conventional: a binary plus SQLite versus a product-specific runtime. That lowers provisioning friction, but Snapback's purpose-built semantics can be a better fit where the benchmark’s custom client and authority boundaries are central. Convex would likely offer a smoother reactive-function workflow and hosted operational story, at the cost of local self-hosted simplicity. Supabase offers the familiar Postgres and SQL ecosystem, which is reassuring for complex reporting and large operational teams, but asks the builder to compose more services. Firebase is similarly strong on managed client integration and realtime primitives, though its rules and data-model tradeoffs can become constraining for relationship-heavy authorization.

A hand-rolled Postgres plus Express service would provide maximum control and make every authority check explicit. It would also require writing, operating, and testing substantially more plumbing. PocketBase’s strongest impression here is that it narrows that initial gap dramatically. Its unresolved question is whether that convenience remains once Clique requires custom revocation, offline ordering, per-recipient media denial, and high-volume paging. This lane has no trustworthy evidence to answer that question, so the experience score below stays conservative.
