# Building Clique with Snapback 4

This was a cold build of a social feed and messenger through accounts, media, groups, ephemeral content, offline writes, and multi-device behavior. I used the two pinned public packages and their published authoring surfaces. I did not inspect another implementation, a Snapback repository, or an earlier benchmark. The comparison below is an engineering interpretation of this build and my general familiarity with other backend architectures, not a controlled performance comparison with freshly built Convex or Supabase versions. I wrote it after M8's passing suite and choreography, before compiling the final measurements.

The most useful property of Snapback 4 was that the backend and the local replica shared a language and an execution model. A message was not independently implemented as a server mutation, a local reducer, a synchronization payload, and an optimistic UI patch. Its table, membership rules, synchronization scope, and mutation lived together in Quarry. That changed the shape of the work. I spent more time expressing the conditions under which an operation was safe to predict, and less time designing a separate reconciliation protocol. Once the compiler accepted those conditions, the offline choreography exercised real durable writes rather than a second application-level queue that happened to resemble the server.

This benefit was clearest in messaging. Group membership controlled access to messages, reactions, and receipt rows. Each send captured its recipient set; later departures stopped counting toward the group receipt requirement. Delivered history could remain on a removed member's device while future delivery ceased. These are related but distinct policies. Keeping their declarations next to each other made it easier to reason about them than spreading them across database policies, subscription filters, local storage, and event handlers. It did not remove application reasoning: I still had to define receipt monotonicity, capture recipients correctly, and avoid counting the sender. The platform supplied machinery, not the product decisions.

Convex is the closest conceptual comparison for the pleasant parts of the server loop. Typed functions, reactive reads, and transactionally consistent application logic can make ordinary collaborative CRUD feel direct. I would expect the online feed and chat screens to be comfortable in that model. Clique's difficult extension is durable disconnected execution with the same authorization-sensitive relationships as the server. A conventional online reactive client still needs a deliberate strategy for surviving process death while offline, predicting mutations, and reconciling rejected writes. Snapback's published local client already owned that strategy. The remaining task was to write mutations it could safely interpret and to present pending and authoritative states correctly.

The compiler made that distinction concrete during push work. I originally placed a profile lookup and preview computation inside a connected-user guard. The operation then could not be predicted. The explanation from `why chat.send` pointed to the relationship between presence and effects. Moving those ordinary computations before the guard left presence deciding only whether to emit the notification. This was a small change with an unusually useful diagnostic: it taught a rule about the execution model, rather than merely reporting a syntax problem. I would otherwise have been tempted to build a separate offline version of send. The refusal kept the implementation unified.

Supabase offers a different attraction: familiar relational storage, SQL, policies, and a broad ecosystem. The underlying relationships in Clique fit a relational database well. I would be comfortable expressing unique DM pairs, membership, reactions, and aggregate counts in that setting. But the application would still need to connect database authorization with realtime delivery, durable local persistence, asset lifecycle, and scheduled work. Those systems can be assembled successfully; the cost is that their guarantees must be composed explicitly. In this build, Quarry rules and synchronization declarations covered much of that composition. The public read-only SQL command remained valuable as an independent inspection surface, particularly when checking scale invariants, without making SQL the application's authoring language.

Assets were another place where integration mattered. Uploading bytes is the easy part of messenger media. The demanding part is deciding who can resolve an attachment, what happens after a tombstone or expiry, whether a cached device retains a reference, and how a one-view image behaves after its first consumption. Snapback's asset references and one-view arm let the backend own meaningful parts of those decisions. I still wrote image and video presentation, picker integration, modal cleanup, and the distinction between ordinary cached media and explicitly opened one-view media. The implementation was smaller than a design in which I also owned signed-link issuance, a deletion service, and consumption bookkeeping, but it was not automatic. Testing old references after deletion was necessary evidence, not a feature inferred from successful upload.

Server time also reduced ambiguity. Edit and delete windows, disappearing messages, and the digest schedule used backend time and fixture-selected configuration. The no-client expiry proof and the scheduled digest proof mattered because a screen timer alone could make an ephemeral message appear correct while leaving it readable on the server. The digest job used an effect identity keyed by user and period. A worker then recorded the provider seam durably. That separation made retries and restart behavior testable without pretending a real push provider had been installed. A production provider is still an integration obligation; the benchmark requested the seam, and that is the boundary this app implements.

The largest client friction was the transition between a known page and an unresolved local observation. An earlier stock paging hook failed while iterating a null result. Later, a second-device reload produced a valid inbox page followed by an incomplete null snapshot. Treating that snapshot as an empty list hid the conversation the choreography needed. I replaced the paging adapter with explicit subscriptions and preserved a known page during this unresolved state, while continuing to accept denied reads and authoritative empty pages. This deserves caution in the comparison: I have evidence of the state transition and of the successful correction, but I did not inspect package internals and cannot confidently assign the entire failure to the platform. A fuller public example covering pagination, route unmounts, and incomplete observations would have reduced the uncertainty.

Quarry itself added learning cost. I tried an unsupported object-spread form, used an empty text match where a browse branch was needed, and initially chose a schedule duration spelling the compiler rejected. Those were authoring mistakes against a new surface, not missing backend capabilities. The guide cards, generated API types, and specific diagnostics generally made recovery quick. The public material also contained examples with differing idioms, so I had to distinguish the current accepted form from a suggestive example. With TypeScript functions or SQL I would have spent less time discovering syntax; with those alternatives I would likely have spent more time assembling the local execution semantics. That tradeoff is the central experience of this build.

Insights showed another benefit of declaring intent close to data. Maintained counts and day buckets made the six lifetime totals and seven daily rows concise. Trending was a ranked server query over a rolling window. The client did not page through the corpus to compute totals. The fresh replay and its checkpoints gave those declarations a much more serious workload than the starter data. Independent counts and named-entity verification are essential here: fast answers on an accidentally underfilled corpus would say little. Read latency measurements in this lane are diagnostic viability evidence on this host, not a cross-backend speed ranking. Additional writes for performance probes belong in a separate copy so they cannot quietly invalidate the fixture being graded.

I cannot honestly report a measured improvement over earlier Snapback generations from this session. The cold-build restriction specifically excluded prior implementations and source trees, and I followed it. My defensible comparison is narrower: the installed fourth-generation surface presents schema, rules, synchronization, prediction, assets, and effects as one authoring system. Any earlier system requiring separate representations for these concerns would impose more coordination work, but that is a conditional architectural comparison, not a claim about an untested release. The final record should not turn that distinction into a fabricated historical result.

What I would carry into another project is the value of a backend that makes local execution a first-class constraint. What I would improve in this project is the client integration layer: more readable component boundaries, explicit handling of incomplete reads, and stronger native runtime evidence beyond a successful Hermes export. The exports demonstrate that the native client arm bundles; they do not demonstrate every picker, asset, or restart journey on a device. Likewise, the local server and logged worker are production-shaped components, not an operated public service with deployment, monitoring, backups, and a real push provider. Snapback reduced the number of independently designed mechanisms needed to reach this shape. It also concentrated unfamiliarity into a language and client state model that deserved careful diagnosis. Both facts belong in the assessment.

The final scale probes changed part of that assessment. Correct fixture counts did not imply acceptable execution cost. An unread-message scan crossed the runtime meter in the busy chat; a maintained receipt count removed that scan. Sequential celebrity writes through the materialized Following path also failed the specified ratio. I chose a read-time Following query and repeated the fresh replay, explicitly checking that the named readers received full pages. That choice shifts work toward reads and carries the compiler's sparse-filter warning; it deserves measured evidence rather than a claim of universal scalability. Removing the populated materialized timeline was refused with `E_TIMELINE_REDEFINE`, so the transition required preserving the old stores and rebuilding fresh state. This late correction is part of the experience, not a footnote that disappears behind the earlier green milestones. It also illustrates a familiar lesson across Convex, Supabase, and other databases: convenient derived state still has a workload-dependent cost, and an aggregate that looks harmless on starter data may need a different representation at scale.

That read-time plan needed one further refinement: scanning wide post rows exhausted the heap meter before filling a sparse reader's page. A compact public event projection made the same membership filter viable at the tested checkpoint. I also learned that an aggregate over receipt rows needs those rows' own expiry policy; hiding the message did not automatically remove its contribution. These corrections increased application-owned bookkeeping. They qualify the earlier praise for integration: the system made limits and execution choices visible, but selecting the right representation remained my responsibility.
