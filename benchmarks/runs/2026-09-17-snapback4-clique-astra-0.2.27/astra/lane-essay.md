# Building Clique on Snapback 4

This build made the backend feel less like a collection of services and more like an executable contract for the application. That mattered most after the first few screens. Authentication, public posts, and a small chat can be implemented in many stacks with little ceremony. Clique became interesting when the same data needed to obey membership rules, arrive on several devices, remain useful offline, expire while no client was running, and support independent clients. Snapback 4 put those concerns close enough together that I could reason about one model rather than maintain several loosely related implementations.

This was a cold build using the exact published packages and their documentation. I did not inspect another implementation, a Snapback source checkout, or a previous run. Comparisons with Convex, Supabase, and conventional server stacks are architectural judgments based on general knowledge, not measurements from an equivalent build performed alongside this one. I have particularly limited grounds for empirical claims about earlier Snapback generations. The useful evidence here is what this release let me express and what I actually had to debug.

The strongest reduction in friction was the connection between schema, authorization, and replication. Membership tables did more than support a server-side access check: their relationship to messages also described which devices could acquire those messages. The local client exposed the same operations and row shapes as the server client. Offline messages, likes, and reactions therefore did not require a separate application-owned command format and reconciliation engine. A durable process-backed replica could close, reopen offline, display pending work, and later settle it. The application still had responsibilities—an offline web shell, correct pending UI, and routes that could resolve without a server—but the queue itself was not another backend project.

In a typical Supabase design, I would expect to make several independent choices: SQL tables and indexes, row-level policies, storage policies, realtime subscriptions, a local database, and an outbox/reconciliation layer. Those choices can be excellent, especially when SQL interoperability and operational familiarity are priorities. Their cost is the number of boundaries that must agree. A deleted message cannot merely disappear from a query; its asset permission and any cached representation must also change. Snapback's asset references and replica rules made that agreement much more direct. Supabase would offer a broader, familiar SQL toolbox, while this build benefited from a narrower system that could reject a contradictory or unjustified operation early.

Convex is a closer comparison in application ergonomics. Its TypeScript functions, generated client surface, transactional mutations, and reactive query model are familiar ways to keep application logic coherent. Quarry required more adjustment than writing ordinary TypeScript. I had to think in index prefixes, bounded pages, explicit rules, and maintained values. On the other hand, the published local/replica arm made offline persistence and prediction part of the same contract I was already using for online behavior. That changed the shape of the work: fewer application-level sync mechanisms, more attention to whether a read or mutation could be justified from the acquired partition.

The constraints were useful, but they were not free. The digest is the clearest example. Its public activity totals fit maintained calendar buckets neatly. Unread messages required a resumable scheduled job with membership and read-state checks. My first cleanup design tried to scan an expiring staging table from a mutation; the compiler rejected that with a specific structural-fanout diagnostic. I changed the staging design and revalidated candidates before emission. A joined-field expression in a rule was also invalid; an indexed exists rule expressed the intended authorization. In SQL I would have had more freedom to write a set-based query. Here I had to expose the work and its bounds, which increased design effort but made the eventual execution model easier to inspect.

Server time was another substantial advantage. Edit and deletion windows were checked by mutations, and message/asset expiry did not depend on a client timer. The isolated proof closed the clients and waited through real deadlines. The scheduled digest similarly ran with no client interaction and survived backend restarts without duplicate period emissions. In a conventional Express/Postgres stack, each of those guarantees is achievable, but I would need to choose and connect a scheduler, transaction boundaries, retries, and asset revocation behavior. Snapback supplied the machinery; my task was still to write the eligibility logic correctly.

The feed recipe was helpful because following feeds are not just a filtered global timeline at the required scale. It gave me a concrete push/pull delivery design, including the celebrity path, instead of inviting an unbounded fan-out or a client-side corpus filter. The later aggregate work was similarly revealing. Lifetime totals and UTC day buckets were short maintain declarations. Trending initially tempted me toward a score-ordered scan with a recent-time filter. The diagnostic explained why old high-score rows could make that scan sparse, so I used a server-expiring rank table. This is a place where the platform changed my implementation rather than merely shortening it.

Not every difficult loop belonged to the backend. The longest avoidable UI investigation came from passing “pending” as the wrong kit prop. Both offline writes existed; their receipt rendered as an empty glyph. I spent time testing reconnection before inspecting the component contract closely enough. Typechecking and reading that prop earlier would have been better engineering. Other corrections concerned navigation, media presentation, a Node-version mismatch in the runner, and restarting a no-watch server after adopting a new backend generation. It would be unfair to report those as platform defects. Conversely, the diagnostics and generated types only help when the engineer actually uses them promptly.

The published surface was broad enough to complete the requested product. I found no required feature that had to be dropped as an inexpressible platform gap. That does not establish that the platform is universally easier or that every operational question is settled. This lane runs a local development binary, logs a push seam rather than delivering production push notifications, and produces Hermes bundles rather than claiming simulator or physical-device verification. The independent client API is documented and exercised, but deployment hardening, long-lived operational monitoring, and store growth over months were not established by this session.

Earlier Snapback generations deserve a restrained comparison. Without importing older implementation details or benchmark memories, I cannot honestly say that a particular old defect disappeared or quantify a generational improvement. What distinguishes the observed Snapback 4 experience is the completeness of the published authoring surface: Quarry rules and sync declarations, executable mutation contracts, server jobs, maintained aggregates, generated client types, and a durable replica arm all participated in one build. Any comparison to an earlier, more online-centered or manually synchronized design should be understood as a contrast in architecture, not a claim that I tested that older release here.

The practical tradeoff is therefore clear. Snapback reduced the amount of independent infrastructure I had to invent, while increasing the importance of expressing access, coverage, and bounded work precisely. Convex would likely feel more immediately familiar at the function-authoring layer; Supabase would offer the strongest connection to a general SQL ecosystem; a hand-built server would maximize control at the cost of owning every seam. For this particular messenger, Snapback's integrated replication, expiry, assets, and scheduling were valuable enough to justify learning Quarry's restrictions. The remaining work was still product engineering: choosing the right read model, exposing truthful UI states, testing authority rather than appearances, and separating actual platform limitations from my own mistakes.

A final visual pass reinforced that distinction: a root-layout parameter hook left a stale tab highlight despite correct navigation. Fixing the hook and rechecking the selected state was ordinary client work. The backend's guarantees did not remove the need to inspect the finished application.

The supplementary scale evidence also places a limit on the performance conclusion. Its data invariants passed, and the aggregate read curves were healthy, but one search midpoint sample missed the instrument's strict interpolation rule. I retained that failed timing predicate rather than selecting a more favorable rerun. Functional completion and a strong architectural fit do not justify claiming that every performance measurement passed.
