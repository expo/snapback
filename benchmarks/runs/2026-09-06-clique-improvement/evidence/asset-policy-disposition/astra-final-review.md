**F1 — Preserve an explicit path out of oversized live assets.** Design locations: [policy-design.md:152](/Users/ccheever/clique-improvement/reviews/asset-grace-policy-final-astra/policy-design.md:152), lines 157–169, 181–189, 231 and 324. Source: `assets_ledger.rs:116–147,214–276,496–537,590–615,648–674`; `asset_http.rs:20–24,563–603,905–991`; `writer_assets.rs:124–184`; LLP 1006 §2a.1 and §8.

The design explicitly exempts live payload, cached rungs and one-use history from custody, then requires their entire footprint to fit the immutable creator's 268,173,312-byte ordinary ceiling when released. It provides no ordinary operation to reduce a claimed asset's accounting footprint. `discard_upload` refuses a claimed ID; normal release, replacement and destructive successor all face the proposed transfer check. Consequently a valid asset can enter a state from which neither its creator nor the system operator can remove its owning reference through a grace-preserving path, even when there is no other garbage and the filesystem works. If the footprint remains oversized after reset's permitted destruction of view history, reset also refuses (F2).

This does not require assuming that twelve 64 MiB rungs are achievable. A small public `once` image with 65,536 distinct authorized viewers has more than 256 MiB of *logical* view charges alone. The source records one row per `(asset_id, principal)`, checks duplicates rather than an audience ceiling, and the proposal leaves growth while live outside custody. This is a structural counterexample, not evidence that any application has that audience. There is also a legacy case without variants or viewers: a previously admitted live original near the old 256 MiB creator limit cannot fit the new limit after base overhead and margin. Grandfathering existing **unlocated** custody in §9 does not grandfather the later transfer of that live asset.

The stated refusal is internally consistent with the chosen invariant. I am not calling it an accidental arithmetic bug. I judge it an unacceptable default for this repair without a deliberate root decision addressing recovery: reads by authorized viewers can change whether a subsequent delete is possible, and waiting, retrying with a new intent, or reducing a batch does not shrink the indivisible asset. The design acknowledges the symptom, but its options compare full future reservation for *every* live asset with release-time refusal; that omits a materially different option.

Viable explicit alternatives include bounding **each asset's eventual transfer footprint**, including future rung/view growth and outstanding completions, while retaining aggregate admission at release. This allows an arbitrarily large live collection with temporary pressure on large releases, without making an individual newly admitted asset intrinsically too large to release. It introduces new admission/delivery refusals and requires a legacy recovery decision; it does not guarantee that a full pool eventually drains under permanent I/O or fork retention. Alternatively, root can approve a larger/provisioned custody budget, or a deliberate destructive disposal route that waives grace for the selected asset and keeps failed deletion charged.

Cached rungs and one-use history need different analysis. Dropping/regenerating derived widths can preserve the original and its full 24-hour reclaim opportunity, but changes LLP 1006 §8's keep-the-rung/one-render commitment, introduces rerender work, and needs immutable-output and failure accounting. It must be an explicit contract change. Dropping view history while allowing the same ID to be reclaimed is incorrect: previous viewers could consume it again. Exact view history needs a real storage allocation, a limit on successful new consumption, or a destructive operation that permanently fences reuse. Merely excluding that history from the custody sum moves the unbounded obligation elsewhere.

**F2 — Treat destructive reset as a separate recovery choice.** Design locations: lines 189, 215, 231, 298, 313 and 324. Source: `writer_assets.rs:8–42`; `assets_ledger.rs:648–662`; `engine/mod.rs:847–861`; `store.rs:858–875`; LLP 1006 §9a, lines 882–892.

Reset already immediately tombstones all asset IDs, clears locators and view history, and uses fork-aware deletion. It does not promise ordinary released-asset grace. Refusing a reset solely because transferring existing live storage would exceed a *garbage* envelope therefore does not follow from the full-grace argument. It follows from the additional choice that every unlocated byte, including a destructive teardown of previously provisioned live data, must fit the same envelope immediately.

For an oversized original or collection of retained variants, no eligible garbage may exist to clean. Smaller operator deletions can help only if every transfer fits and capacity can actually be recovered. They cannot repair F1, and fork-held tombstones can prevent capacity recovery indefinitely. The reset refusal is an explicit coherent simplification, but it removes the ordinary development/recovery escape precisely when it is most useful. View-heavy assets deserve one qualification: current reset deletes `_asset_views`, so a reset calculation using final state need not retain that history. The reset policy must specify this rather than treating all releases identically.

A viable alternative is a fenced, resumable destructive teardown within the existing owner/reset operation: invalidate old delivery/claim authority, stop positive asset allocation, process finite pages of pre-existing data, retain failed-delete ownership, and resume after a crash. Its temporary accounting bound would be the measured pre-reset retained footprint plus bounded scratch, monotonically non-increasing, rather than 512 MiB regardless of already-live storage. This **explicitly changes the all-unlocated envelope during teardown**; it is not a way to pretend oversized garbage fits that limit. Ordinary releases can still receive all 24 hours. Existing snapshot referrers must remain protected and separately accounted for; reset cannot promise to free snapshot-owned keys. Root may instead choose advance live-storage reservations or retain reset refusal, but the design should compare these costs explicitly. I do not approve either alternative on root's behalf.

**F3 — Admit reset before any destructive owner-side work.** Design locations: lines 187–189, 222, 231 and 298. Source: `writer_assets.rs:8–22`; `blob_store.rs:171–203`; `engine/mod.rs:847–915`; `registry.rs:938–949`.

The proposed common final-transaction check cannot by itself make a rejected reset atomic. `State::reset` calls `self.staging.clear()` at line 10, before `reset_and_seed_at` enters the core transaction. `LocalStaging::clear` directly unlinks staging files. Consider an in-flight receiving upload or a paused finalizer plus enough live originals to cause the new reset check to refuse: the database rolls back, but staging files are already gone. A running worker may fail after a refusal that supposedly preserved custody. SQL rollback cannot restore those bytes.

The old ordering is inherited; adding a normal capacity-rejection branch exposes it on the newly specified atomic-refusal path. The design needs to require that capacity admission and reversible reset preparation precede any staging unlink or cancellation, with admitted cleanup handed to the same tracked work ownership used for discard/GC. A read-only preflight alone is insufficient if relevant state can change before the authoritative transaction. Preserve the check inside the writer transaction and make the physical ordering explicit. The future test should hold both a receiving body and finalizer at barriers, reject reset for capacity, and assert unchanged file availability, ledger facts, live references, and continued worker behavior—not just unchanged application rows.

**F4 — Redact capacity detail consistently across all response paths.** Design locations: lines 245–260. Source: `writer_assets.rs:187–209`; `assets_ledger.rs:214–230`; `writer_wire.rs:25–34`; `offline.rs:76–97`; `jobs.rs:52–64,119–155`.

§8 requires structured scope, usage, limit/headroom and resource attribution. It separately forbids foreign creators' exact usage and owner-wide exact activity totals. These cannot both hold if headroom is exact for the failing scope. `usage = limit - headroom` reveals the forbidden number even when a response labels its other usage field as belonging to the caller. This matters when an authorized moderator releases another creator's asset and when a fresh principal encounters owner pressure. Redacting only IDs and the field named `usage` does not suffice.

Specify a public projection: exact caller-owned figures, the configured limit and request facts that are safe to disclose, plus pressure/recovery booleans for foreign/owner scopes; omit or coarsen their headroom, deficit and equivalent reconstructable totals. Preserve that same redacted historical terminal in replay, rather than querying later state. Operator-only diagnostics can retain fuller details. This is a contract inconsistency to resolve, not a claim that the unimplemented code has already leaked data. Also include the parked-job conversion in the implementation scope: `JobRefusal::rejected` currently drops specialized detail and reconstructs a generic family/guide, independently of the two wire converters named in §8.

**F5 — Specify how the entire sweep yields and progresses.** Design locations: lines 149–155, 213, 219–225, 277–281 and 287–309. Source: `assets_ledger.rs:395–399,449–473`; `writer_assets.rs:62–118`; `asset_gc.rs:16–99`; `blob_store.rs:307–397`; `writer.rs:504–513`.

The promised bounded pages/turns and continued sibling progress are not supplied by the current callback enumeration. The writer first materializes **all** live-ledger IDs, including unlimited claimed application storage. `fork_all_references` materializes all IDs from every fork. `enumerate` then walks the entire blob directory synchronously; it has no continuation cursor. One `delete` error aborts the enumeration and prevents the caller from finishing that sweep's successful siblings. The same full sweep runs before the writer serves requests at boot. A page limit on the reclaim candidate query alone changes none of this. An index on unlocated rows does not bound the live/fork sets.

This is inherited work and memory exposure, not proof of a measured regression. But the proposal now relies on cleanup for successful ordinary deletion and promises bounded turns and failure isolation as part of its stronger invariant. Give the concrete continuation/ownership shape, per-turn work limit, per-ID completion/error handling, and treatment of changing fork referrers and startup reconstruction. For example, bounded candidate-key cleanup with indexed referrer checks plus a separately continued orphan-directory walk can avoid rebuilding the full set on every page; it still needs an explicit fork/iterator lifetime and crash story. Existing deterministic rung slots can bound keys per candidate. Merely changing order to “cleanup first” risks repeatedly visiting failed early IDs unless continuation advances past failures.

The new SQL sums also deserve an explicit cost statement. Partial indexes can restrict sums to unlocated rows, but do not make them constant-time: the ordinary pool permits up to 65,504 base IDs owner-wide, and grandfathered stores can be larger. View-count reconstruction spans live history as well. The matrix's small/scaled cases can test arithmetic, paging and races, but do not themselves establish latency or memory behavior at those cardinalities. Its 100-*ordinary*-row limit need not prevent a separate large released ledger or long view history; specify that cost validation explicitly rather than treating scaled arithmetic as its substitute. No such pass is claimed here. Resolve the algorithmic scope now and leave actual qualification explicitly pending.

**Finite accounting is not a measured physical budget.** The ordinary ceilings, identity maxima (65,504/32,736), uniform 158-byte count (64,264), and `6,204 + 40×8,192 = 333,884` calculation are arithmetically correct. The creator payload headroom is reduced by 270,336 bytes. Five 50 MiB originals per creator and ten split across creators fit only absent other charge, as stated. The fixed 4 KiB units bound logical cardinality; the asserted fixture does not prove worst-case SQLite pages/indexes, WAL growth, filesystem allocation or metadata amplification.

Twelve slots and a 64 MiB output ceiling are present in source, but `12×64 MiB` is a loose product of ceilings, not an observed or demonstrated realizable retained-image footprint. The helper also limits pixels and edges (`media.rs:26–31,276–308`). It is right to account for actual retained rung sizes rather than silently treating that product as either routine demand or a reservation needed for every image.

Scratch is a separate, substantial obligation. There are real input, strip, normalized, rung-source, rung-output, helper-internal and publish temporary paths. Helper temporaries currently use process/sequence names and ignore unlink failure on drop (`media.rs:885–931`); strip and publish have similar cleanup paths. Associating all of these with an asset and retaining a physical-work slot after failure requires changes across the helper/file interfaces, not only a ledger field. Source-copy creation occurs **before** the two-helper permit (`asset_http.rs:950–970`), so two helpers do not imply two source copies. The design correctly identifies this.

For scale only, substituting `I=256 MiB` into the design's deliberately loose `4×(4+64)×max(I,64 MiB)` scratch expression yields **68 GiB** of named scratch allowance, before separately retained open streams. This is arithmetic on the proposed expression, not a claim that 68 GiB is achievable, provisioned, or acceptable. Existing worker counts and a finite expression do not prove a useful physical limit. A concrete file-lifetime enumeration and target-filesystem measurements are still required. If failed work retains actual HTTP permits, clarify the availability cost: ordinary asset delivery shares the JSON worker class (`serve.rs:154–175,203–242`). Retrying cleanup must not accidentally require a permit permanently held by that same failed work.

**Proportionality and the decision root actually faces.** The demonstrated source issue is a small pending-object quota also counting successful releases. A count-predicate separation fixes that behavior. It does not establish a bound on release garbage, and the published source did not have such an enforced aggregate bound to preserve. Conversely, it is unsound to call the predicate-only change a complete physical-custody repair. These are two different scopes root can choose explicitly.

These changes can be worthwhile for reliable app behavior, but “same nominal 256/512 MiB” is not evidence of unchanged compatibility, unchanged implementation cost, or unchanged physical allocation. The ledger changes also add persistent metadata to live application assets, outside `C`. Shared bytes deliberately leave cross-creator upload pressure possible after the count fix. Fork-blocked cleanup can permanently consume that shared allowance. No workload evidence here establishes whether these costs are proportionate.

The incompatible guarantees should be recorded precisely: unlimited **immediately successful** transfers from arbitrarily large live storage, full retention of every transferred footprint for 24 hours, and a fixed finite unlocated budget cannot all hold. One can limit transfer throughput, reserve aggregate future release space, expand the provisioned budget, or explicitly shorten/waive retention. A per-asset transfer limit is another useful choice, but does not promise unlimited aggregate release throughput. A fenced destructive reset can preserve ordinary 24-hour grace while choosing a different, monotonically shrinking teardown bound. Regenerating rungs changes their retention/compute contract; erasing once history while keeping reclaimability breaks semantics. None of these alternatives is silently substituted for the reviewed design.

Root should resolve F1/F2 as product choices, fix F3/F4 as contract/ordering defects, and make F5's bounded-work mechanism concrete. The existing invariants, offline fidelity and full grace should remain explicit during that revision. Runtime and physical qualification remain future work regardless of the policy choice; this review supplies no passing claim for either.
