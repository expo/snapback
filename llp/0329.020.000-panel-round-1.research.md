## Fable

# Panel round 1 — Fable 5 (orchestrator; not an independent voice)

**Declared bias:** I wrote the counter-proposal on the table. Discount accordingly.
What follows is my best argument for it, plus the strongest case I can make
*against* it, because a panelist who only argues one side is not useful.

## 1. Which architecture

**Aquifer stays the tier; Snapback 2's client becomes the reference rich provider.**

The founder's proposal fails on a structural fact rather than a taste question:
`ClientStore::hydrate(schema, receipt)` requires a closure receipt and installs
*verdicts*. Snapback 2's client is not a general local-first engine that happens to
speak Snapback — it is the server's interpreter, running on the other side of a
wire, fed by an artifact only that server can produce. Point it at Rails and each
capability falls in turn: no receipt → no completeness; no program IR → no local
re-execution → no prediction, no offline recompute; no verdicts → no local
authorization; no read-set validation → no OCC. The residue is a row cache with an
outbox, which is a fine thing but is not what makes Snapback 2 interesting.

The founder's real objective — *one data story per app* — is achievable without
moving the tier. What it needs is a seam expressive enough that a rich provider can
say what it knows, which Aquifer's current "rows and writes" cannot.

## 2. Is "proved vs asserted" sound, or does it reproduce the defect?

This is the question I am least sure about and the one the panel should spend its
time on.

**The case that it is sound.** The distinction is real and irreducible: a server
that executed your program can prove coverage; a bridge that fetched some rows can
only claim it. Refusing to represent a real distinction does not make it go away —
it makes it invisible, which is precisely the failure this project already
documented at length.

**The case that it reproduces the defect.** LLP 0329's finding was not "there were
two tiers." It was three specific properties: the boundary was *implicit*, it was
*determined by an incidental choice*, and it *degraded silently*. A backend tier
avoids all three only if the grade is (a) declared, (b) visible in the type of what
you get back, and (c) impossible to consume without acknowledging. If
`useQuery` returns the same shape either way and the grade is a field you may
ignore, we have rebuilt the defect with better documentation.

**My position:** sound *only* if the grade is in the result type, not in a field.
A thin provider should not return `Rows` with `completeness: unknown` — it should
return a type that has no `complete` case to read, so code that requires
completeness fails to compile against it. That is the difference between a mode and
a contract, and it is the same rule that made the Contract-block front end refuse
eight of eight bad first attempts at parse rather than at runtime.

If that is too strict to ship, then I would rather have **one grade** — every
provider asserted, no proofs anywhere — than two grades distinguished by a field.
A uniformly weaker system is honest. A silently bifurcated one is what we are
trying to escape.

## 3. Cost of being wrong

**Wrong in my direction** (Aquifer stays the tier, Snapback is a provider): the
cost is duplicated machinery — two stores, two staleness notions, two eviction
policies — and a seam that keeps needing widening. Annoying, incremental,
*recoverable*: each widening is additive and can be done later.

**Wrong in the founder's direction** (Snapback 2's client becomes the tier): the
cost is that the tier grows to cover route loaders, response caches, secure
storage, pooled sessions, window restoration and cross-runtime transport — all of
Aquifer's scope — while carrying a receipt-shaped core that foreign backends
cannot satisfy. That is not incremental; it is a rewrite discovered late, and the
sunk position is a data tier that is excellent for one backend and mediocre for
every other.

**So the asymmetry favours my direction**, and I would weight that heavily. But I
note the failure mode of my own position is the more *insidious* one: duplicated
machinery is exactly how you get four documents lawfully disagreeing, which is the
condition Aquifer was written to end.

## 4. Is there a version of the founder's idea that keeps its appeal?

Yes, and I think it is the most interesting thing in this brief.

The appeal is "local-first regardless of backend." The obstacle is that Snapback 2
earns local-first by having the server prove things. But **a bridge author could
supply the missing declarations themselves**: a schema, row rules, how to fetch
rows for a query, how to submit a write. Then the client genuinely re-executes
locally-declared programs, with the foreign backend as a *row source* rather than
an authority. That is approximately Replicache's pull/push model and it ships
today in other systems.

What it buys: real local re-execution and prediction against Rails.
What it costs: authorization becomes client-declared and therefore
presentation-only; conflicts fall back to last-write-wins or an app-defined merge;
completeness is asserted by the bridge author.

That is a *legitimate product tier*. My objection is not to its existence but to
its packaging. It should be reached by writing a visibly different thing — not by
passing a different URL to the same constructor.

## What would change my mind

If someone shows that the closure receipt can be **synthesised at the bridge** for
a useful class of foreign backends — say, a bridge that declares its own row rules
and computes coverage from a declared pagination contract — then the gap between
rich and thin narrows from *kind* to *degree*, and the founder's architecture gets
much stronger. I do not think that is impossible; I have not thought hard enough
about it to rule it out, and it is the thing I would most like the other families
to attack.

## Codex

# Panel round 1 — Codex

## Position

Neither proposal is right as written. I would keep **Aquifer as Exact's public data tier** and put the Snapback client behind it as a rich execution provider, but I would reject the counter-proposal's simple “rich versus thin” backend grading and its `complete | partial | unknown` field as sufficient.

Aquifer should own the semantics that are genuinely backend-independent: authored cells/resources, identity and principal isolation, dependency scheduling, request lifecycle, route/server integration, replay into views, and the view-visible state machine. Snapback should own the semantics that arise from the bounded program protocol: the receipt-seeded row store, installed verdicts, range and absence coverage, exact program re-execution, prediction, OCC, and reconnect reconciliation. A foreign provider should own whatever narrower guarantees it can actually supply.

That is composition, not two competing data tiers. It does require an explicit ownership rule: materialization covered by a provider's evidence belongs to that provider. Aquifer may declare persistence/retention intent, but it must not independently evict rows while a Snapback receipt still claims their range is held. Likewise, Aquifer's optimistic cell overlay should display a Snapback-produced prediction, not compute a second prediction beside it.

The code makes this boundary fairly stark. `ClientStore::hydrate` does not accept generic rows: it accepts a `ClosureReceipt`, seeds its rows, installs its verdicts, and retains its range and absence evidence. Re-execution compares exact program-site/prefix traces against that evidence. A rows-and-writes Rails adapter has only three choices: leave coverage unknown and lose the defining behavior, invent coverage and risk a false `Fresh`, or implement the Snapback protocol in substance. The third is not a thin bridge.

I would therefore amend Aquifer's provider seam now, while it is already breaking, but with a **typed evidence and capability channel**, not merely a completeness bit. Aquifer need not understand Snapback's receipt internally; it must be able to preserve the provider's evidence and project its verified outcomes into separate cell axes. Lifecycle, connection staleness, coverage, prediction, and authorization provenance should not be collapsed into one status.

## “Proved versus asserted”

It is a sound distinction, but not a sound two-grade taxonomy for whole backends.

First, “proved” needs a narrow meaning. A Snapback receipt is protocol-backed evidence that a particular bounded program's trace is covered under a particular schema, verdict set, and authorization epoch. From the material I read, I would call that **receipt-backed or trace-certified**, not claim that it is a cryptographic proof. It also does not mean eternally current. A Rails endpoint can honestly assert that one response contains every row the endpoint chose to return, but that assertion does not establish negative knowledge, closure after local mutations, evaluator equivalence, or local authorization semantics.

Second, capabilities vary by operation, not just provider. The useful dimensions include at least:

- whether this result carries checkable coverage/absence evidence;
- whether the same program can be re-executed locally;
- whether writes can be predicted and reconciled under the same semantics;
- whether authorization outcomes have an authoritative epoch and fail-closed refresh path;
- whether offline data is merely cached, or is sufficient for complete recomputation.

A single “rich backend” badge will eventually hide exceptions. A rich provider can also return `Partial` for a particular receipt. Conversely, one carefully mirrored Rails resource might support stronger guarantees than the rest of that Rails application.

The authoring rule should be: **the declaration states the guarantees it requires; the provider declaration states the capabilities it offers; the compiler/admission step checks the match.** For example, a resource that depends on offline-complete replay and predicted writes should visibly require those capabilities. It must fail to bind to a row-only provider; it must not silently become a weaker resource. A resource intentionally accepting remote-only semantics should say so, and its result type/view grammar must require handling `unknown` or incomplete coverage.

The exact spelling is secondary, but it cannot be an identical `useQuery` whose meaning changes with deployment configuration. Either use visibly different declarations such as `local resource` and `remote resource`, or one `resource` declaration with an unavoidable `requires` clause. Generated APIs should carry the distinction in their types. Runtime evidence then reports whether the promised mechanism produced `covered`, `partial(site IDs/reason)`, or `unknown` for this execution. Static capability and dynamic outcome are different facts; both are needed.

An arbitrary provider-supplied `complete` value must not normalize to the same state as receipt-backed coverage. Aquifer already has the right precedent in its payload design: an audience label is not a proof, and missing proof fails closed. Apply the same rule here. “Asserted complete” may be useful metadata, but it cannot unlock local-complete rendering, prediction, or authorization behavior reserved for admitted evidence.

This means tiering does not itself reproduce the old defect. **Invisible, ambient tiering does.** Capability requirements visible in the declaration, enforced before execution, and reflected in the value type avoid that failure mode.

## Cost of choosing wrong

Choosing the founder's architecture and being wrong is the more dangerous and less recoverable mistake. It would put Snapback-specific concepts—programs, certificates, verdicts, coverage sites, receipt identity, and reconciliation—behind an API advertised as backend-neutral. Thin adapters would then either expose obvious feature gaps or, worse, make semantically stronger claims than their backends justify. False completeness can render a partial set as whole; divergent prediction can accept an offline edit that the real backend interprets differently; mishandled authorization evidence can expose cached material. Later correction means splitting an apparently uniform API across every authored resource, migrating persisted state and wire contracts, and auditing assumptions already embedded in apps. Architectural migration is expensive; incorrect data or authorization already shown to users cannot be repaired retroactively.

Choosing Aquifer and being wrong has real costs: an extra engine boundary, provider authoring work, possible double caching, duplicated optimistic machinery, frame-budget pressure, and a risk of reducing Snapback to the lowest common denominator. Those are not small costs. But they are mostly recoverable if ownership is settled now and the rich evidence is not discarded. Aquifer can become thinner, delegate storage and prediction to Snapback, or eventually collapse around a de facto single provider while preserving the app declarations. The reverse migration—from a universal Snapback-shaped public contract back to honest heterogeneous semantics—is much harder.

The qualification is important: a narrow rows-only Aquifer seam that strips receipts and then rebuilds its own store, completeness model, and prediction layer would be the worst of both directions. I would not accept Aquifer without the rich-provider amendment and the single-owner rules above.

## The founder's appeal, preserved honestly

There is a viable version of “one data story for every app,” but the uniformity should be in **authoring and cell consumption**, not in pretending every backend supplies the same knowledge.

Every Exact app can use Aquifer resources, cells, persistence policies, offline launch behavior, write receipts, and view grammar. Snapback can be the default bundled provider and make the strong path cheap. A foreign backend gets one of two integrations:

1. A thin provider supplies rows and writes. It can support cached offline reads, queued writes, and explicitly speculative overlays, but the app sees that coverage is unproven and authorization is server-only. This is offline-tolerant, not offline-computable.
2. A substantial compatibility gateway implements the Snapback semantic contract in front of the foreign system. It needs an authoritative snapshot/changefeed, stable identity and versioning, write interception or equivalent semantics, authorization integration, and the ability to issue genuine coverage/verdict receipts. It must pass the same conformance obligations as a Snapback server. Then the client can honestly use the rich path. At that point the foreign system is behind a Snapback-compatible server boundary, not behind a client-side row bridge.

That preserves the founder's useful instinct: local execution should be the paved road and should not require each app to assemble a cache, outbox, and optimistic layer. It rejects only the impossible part—that a client adapter can manufacture server knowledge. The product can promise one data model everywhere and a standard upgrade path to strong local-first behavior. It should not promise identical local-first guarantees for an arbitrary backend that has not supplied the facts those guarantees require.

My main uncertainty is implementation cost: the inspected `ClientStore` is a compact prototype and does not include subscription management, persistence, outbox settlement, or the full bridge protocol. That could change the relative engineering cost. It does not change the semantic boundary: rows alone cannot recover verdicts, negative knowledge, or program-closure evidence.

## Grok

# Client data tier: do not unify the spelling

**Position:** Reject both proposals as stated. Snapback 2’s client is the data tier for server-derived data that can carry a receipt. Aquifer must not be that tier, and Snapback must not pretend to be backend-neutral. Foreign backends are a *different surface with different types*, not a thinner provider and not a Snapback “bridge.” The load-bearing mistake in both proposals is the same: one authoring spelling for two computational models.

The orchestrator’s counter-proposal is the more dangerous of the two. It sounds like honesty (completeness on the wire, two provider grades) while reproducing the documented defect in a more respectable shape.

---

## 1. Which architecture

Neither.

**Snapback 2’s client is the architecture for proved, re-executable data.** That follows from what it is. Hydration is `ClientStore::hydrate(schema, receipt)` plus `install_verdicts`. Local state is `Fresh | Predicted | Partial(exact site ids)`. The client runs the same bounded program the server runs. Those are not cache features. They are the store. A provider that emits `rows-arrived` cannot be the home of that model without flattening it into rows and then trying to smuggle the rest back in as metadata.

**Aquifer, as currently scoped, is a second data tier occupying the same job.** Caching, staleness, persistence, replay, optimistic overlays — that overlap is not layering. It is two state machines for one kind of fact. “Snapback is a provider behind Aquifer; Exact’s knowledge of any backend ends at rows and writes” is false for Snapback and, if enforced, destructive. Snapback’s knowledge *begins* after rows: programs, sites, verdicts, predicted-versus-fresh, partiality by site id. If Exact is forbidden to know that, Exact cannot use Snapback as Snapback.

**Foreign backends do not belong behind either abstraction under the same call.** A bridge cannot manufacture a receipt. Pointing Snapback’s client at Rails leaves a row cache and an outbox wearing Snapback’s clothes. Putting Rails behind Aquifer as a “thin provider” that reports `unknown` leaves a cell that cannot predict, cannot prove coverage, and cannot authorize locally — still addressed with the same hook.

What I would actually do:

- **Default Exact 2 path:** author against Snapback 2’s client. The server is a Snapback 2 server. Offline, prediction, local auth, OCC are properties of that pair. Persistence and restoration live in that store, not as Aquifer cell policies sitting on top of a row feed.
- **Foreign path:** a separately named, separately typed API that fetches and writes, maybe caches, and *does not claim* prediction, proved completeness, or local authorization. It should feel worse. That is the point.
- **Aquifer shrinks or dies for this job.** If it survives, it is a binding/subscription layer for things that are *not* this: component-adjacent shared async, route-scoped non-server state, truly external feeds with no Exact semantics. It does not own optimistic overlays or offline recompute for server-derived rows. The constraint “Aquifer must never block on Snapback” is a confession that Aquifer thinks it is the coordinator. For proved data it is not.

The founder’s instinct that Exact 2 should have *one* local-first client is right for Exact 2 apps talking to Exact’s model. The error is “or Rails via a bridge you write.” The orchestrator’s instinct that a bridge cannot mint a receipt is right. The error is keeping Aquifer as the tier and encoding the difference as provider grade.

---

## 2. “Proved vs asserted” is sound as a type, not as a tier

Proved versus asserted is a real distinction. Snapback can *prove* coverage and authorization outcomes because the receipt carries verdicts and the client re-runs the program. A Rails adapter can only *assert* that the rows it returned are the rows. Those are not two qualities of the same object.

**It is not a sound way to *tier backends*,** and **putting it on the provider seam reproduces the defect.**

`complete | partial{reason} | unknown` on `rows-arrived` is a runtime tag on a shared cell. Authors still write one spelling. The engine can see the difference; the authoring site cannot. That is the defect with a new payload: capability that depends on something other than the form you typed, invisible where you typed it. The 74% of writes that escaped did not escape because the runtime lacked a completeness enum. They escaped because the convenient spelling was available and the real capability was elsewhere.

Worse, the enum is already a lossy projection of Snapback. `Partial(exact site ids)` is actionable. `partial{reason}` is a comment. `unknown` is a shrug. Once Rails reports `unknown` and Snapback reports `complete` through the same cell type, policies (offline, restoration, retry) will either:

- refuse to act on `unknown`, in which the thin provider does not participate in the local-first story you sold, or
- act on `unknown` anyway, in which you have asserted completeness in the only place that matters — what the UI is allowed to do.

Tiering by backend (rich Snapback provider / thin Rails provider) also misplaces the cut. A Snapback query can be `Partial`. A Snapback write can fail OCC. Proof is a property of a *result*, not of a vendor. If the authoring API is `useQuery` in both cases, people will choose by which backend they have, not by which guarantee they need, and they will not be told they chose.

**How to make it unmissable:** different types, different names, no implicit coercion.

- Proved data is a Snapback query/mutation type that only constructs from a compiled program plus a receipt-shaped contract. The hook does not exist for a URL.
- Asserted data is a foreign fetch/write type with no `Predicted`, no verdicts, no `Fresh` that means what Snapback means. Cache freshness, if any, is named something else (`Cached`, `Stale`, `Failed`).
- You cannot pass asserted data into a component that requires proved data without an explicit, ugly adapter that returns `unknown` *and changes the type*. Not a completeness field. A different type.

If that seems inconvenient, it is working. Convenience at the call site is how the last opaque tier ate 74% of writes.

I am guessing at Exact’s concrete hook names; the requirement is the type split, not the identifiers.

---

## 3. Cost of being wrong

The unrecoverable move in both directions is **freezing a unified authoring API before the guarantees are the same.** Migrations can replace engines. They cannot cheaply split `useQuery` after every screen has used it for both proved and asserted data.

### Founder wrong (Snapback client as the tier; bridges behind it)

**What happens:** Docs, examples, and every query look local-first. Rails bridges do not re-execute, do not install verdicts, do not prove coverage. Prediction and offline become properties of *which bridge you happened to point at*, invisible at the call site. You have shipped the central defect as the default architecture, with Snapback’s prestige as camouflage.

**Why it is hard to unwind:** The client API *promises* Snapback. Every app is written as if receipts exist. To tell the truth later you must either break the API or add the type split after the ecosystem is full of the unified spelling — the same retrofit that failed last time.

**Recoverable only if** the “bridge” is not a client adapter at all, but a Snapback *server* in front of foreign storage (see §4). Then you were never wrong about the client; you were wrong about the word “bridge.”

### Orchestrator wrong (Aquifer as tier; Snapback as rich provider)

**What happens:** Two state machines. Snapback’s `Fresh | Predicted | Partial(sites)` gets projected into cell state plus `rows-arrived` completeness. Optimistic overlays exist in the evaluator and again as cell policy. Persistence is a cell policy over a store that already hydrates from receipts. The 7.8 KB byte-identical evaluator becomes a plugin behind an event set that cannot express sites or verdicts without further widening — and each widening makes the shared spelling more plausible.

**Authoring lock-in:** Exact 2 apps author against Aquifer. Snapback’s defining API never becomes the app-facing one. Promoting Snapback later is a rewrite of every query, plus a fight with cell policies that have grown around “never block on Snapback.”

**This is the more likely wrong,** because it feels like architecture: seams, grades, visible cell state, backend-neutrality. It is how you lose the product while keeping the diagram.

**Recoverable if** Aquifer has not yet become the stable authoring surface — it is Draft, the provider channel is already breaking. That recoverability expires the moment Exact 2 apps are taught `useQuery` against a provider.

### Which mistake is worse

**Unifying the spelling is worse than picking the “wrong” engine.** A Snapback-only Exact 2 that cannot talk to Rails without a real Snapback server is a product limitation you can extend. A unified tier that lies about proof is a category error you will document, then paper with completeness flags, then rediscover in the next postmortem.

Between the two written proposals, **the orchestrator’s is the one I would attack first.** It keeps the hazard and adds a translation layer. The founder’s is wrong about foreign backends but right that Snapback’s client is not “a provider.”

## 4. The founder’s idea, without the hazard

The appeal is not “talk to Rails.” The appeal is: **every Exact 2 app has one local-first data story, and the client is a replica of the program, not a cache of the last JSON.**

Keep that. Drop the client-side bridge.

**Local-first regardless of backend is true only if “backend” means “storage/source behind a Snapback 2 server.”** A foreign system that wants Exact’s client properties runs (or embeds) Snapback 2. Rails is persistence or a source the *server* compiles into programs and receipts. The client still hydrates from a receipt. Completeness is still proved. Authorization is still local. No one writes a browser adapter that invents verdicts.

That is more work than a thin provider. It is the only work that preserves the defining property.

What you then *do not* do:

- Do not give raw HTTP the Snapback client.
- Do not give it Aquifer cells that look like Snapback cells.
- Do not sell “one `useQuery`.” Sell one *proved* path, and a second path that cannot be mistaken for it.

If Exact cannot staff Snapback adapters for the long tail of Rails/GraphQL, the honest product is: Exact 2 is local-first on Snapback; other backends are fetch-and-cache and look like it. That is a smaller story than the founder’s pitch and a truer one than the orchestrator’s two grades. I would rather have a visible hole in the product than a capability that depends on authoring form.

A weaker salvage — Snapback client always, but foreign mode is a *different type* inside that client (`UnprovedStore`, no `install_verdicts`, no `Predicted`) — still requires the type split. At that point you have not replaced Aquifer with Snapback; you have put two stores in one package. Fine as a packaging choice. Not an architecture that “unifies the moving parts.”

---

## Direct answers

1. **Architecture:** Snapback 2 client is the tier for receipt-backed data. Aquifer is not that tier. Foreign backends are not providers of that tier. Do not unify them.
2. **Proved vs asserted:** Sound as distinct types at the authoring site. Unsound as backend grades or as a completeness enum on a shared cell. Runtime visibility is how the last defect hid.
3. **Cost:** Unified spelling is the unrecoverable mistake in both directions. Wrong engine with honest types is recoverable. Orchestrator-wrong also burns the Draft window; once apps author to Aquifer, Snapback cannot surface.
4. **Founder without hazard:** Every Exact 2 app uses Snapback 2 on the client *and* talks Snapback on the wire. Foreign storage sits behind a Snapback server, not behind a client bridge. Anything else is a second, uglier API.

The next time someone proposes that the difference be “visible in cell state,” treat that as the defect proposing itself.
