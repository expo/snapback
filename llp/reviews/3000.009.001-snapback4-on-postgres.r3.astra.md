Concurrent copy also needs an explicit treatment of constraints across records. S0 supports commissioning the sizing work for (a), but does not establish its production throughput. This review covers `c24adea5bc`; the checkout remains unchanged, and implementation-level graduation and managed-provider failover are **UNVERIFIED**.

## New fundamental and major findings

### N1. The continuity checks do not constrain which replica may be promoted — FUNDAMENTAL — child A §5.4

[A:330](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:330) requires synchronous commits, a verified synchronous standby, and fencing of the old primary. Those conditions are insufficient.

Consider a primary with standby S1 acknowledging synchronous commits and another replica S2 lagging behind. A revocation commits synchronously to S1. The provider then fences the primary and promotes S2. All three stated conditions held, but the promoted database lacks the acknowledged revocation. A nonempty `synchronous_standby_names` does not establish promotion eligibility, and a timeline change detects the transition without proving continuity.

**Recommended direction:** require a provider/failover protocol that promotes only a replica containing the acknowledged durable prefix, including across changes of synchronous membership. Bind the continuity decision to that guarantee and the promoted target, rather than inferring it from replication settings and primary fencing.

The experiment does not establish this property: real provider failover is explicitly unrun ([fencing README:476](../../snapback4/experiments/postgres-fencing/README.md:476)). W9 uses a nonexistent synchronous standby and runs the takeover with `synchronous_commit=local` ([witness.rs:860](../../snapback4/experiments/postgres-fencing/src/bin/witness.rs:860)); it demonstrates ambiguous local commitment, not preservation through promotion.

### N2. An abort transaction can commit successfully after losing the decision — FUNDAMENTAL — child B §5

The handoff explicitly requires its conditional update to affect one row ([B:212](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:212)). The abort paragraph instead authorizes reopening SQLite after the abort transaction’s **commit succeeds**, without requiring that it established `decision = 'abort'` ([B:223](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:223)).

The failing execution is:

1. Handoff commits `decision = 'commit'` and makes Postgres live; its reply is lost.
2. The timeout path executes the conditional abort update.
3. That update affects zero rows. Its transaction nevertheless commits successfully.
4. The stated abort procedure reopens SQLite.

The decision row correctly prevents two decisions, but successful `COMMIT` does not prove which decision won. The recovery bullets are stronger than the abort algorithm.

**Recommended direction:** require an affected-row/`RETURNING` check and authoritative confirmation of `abort` for the exact source-prepared attempt. Zero affected rows must resolve through the recovery rules; a committed handoff keeps the source fenced. Apply this symmetrically to reversal.

### N3. The interim worker fence still misses an expired, executing handler — MAJOR — child A §4.3; child B §2

The planned shared attempt protocol addresses the previous finding. Its interim substitute does not: graduation and promotion refuse only for a `running` effect or a local completion associated with a nonterminal effect ([A:201](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:201), [B:49](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:49)).

A healthy handler can still be executing when its lease expires. `expire_run` changes its state to `queued` and clears the claim ([effects.rs:513](../../snapback4/crates/snapback4-core/src/engine/effects.rs:513)). Meanwhile, the local ledger inserts its completion only **after** the handler returns ([worker-ledger.ts:54](../../snapback4/packages/snapback4/src/worker-ledger.ts:54)). Both interim refusal predicates are therefore false. A successor on another host can claim the queued effect and invoke the handler while the original call remains active.

**Recommended direction:** make the shared attempt protocol a prerequisite for cross-host promotion/graduation with previously started nonterminal effects, or define a quiescence protocol that preserves the local ledger’s exclusion and parks/reconciles uncertain attempts. Waiting for lease expiry cannot establish quiescence.

### N4. Per-key ordering needs a constraint-safe import mechanism — MAJOR — child B §§4.2–4.3

The per-key version rule correctly prevents a stale chunk from overwriting a newer image. It does not establish that all intermediate images can be applied through the family imports ([B:112](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:112), [B:143](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:143)).

For example, `_credentials` has a primary key on principal and a separate unique email constraint ([store.rs:299](../../snapback4/crates/snapback4-core/src/store.rs:299)). Suppose the captured history inserts principal B with email E, then replaces B with principal A using E—the replacement case already included in [B:106](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:106). A later chunk can install A before replay reaches B’s earlier insert.

A target import enforcing email uniqueness now rejects B’s insert. B has no newer per-key version yet, so the specified rule cannot skip it; ordered replay cannot reach the later deletion that would remove the conflict. Retrying the same insert does not help. Foreign-key dependencies also require treatment when families represent different source points.

**Recommended direction:** specify whether import uses unconstrained staging records or another protocol that permits these intermediate states, and how it establishes the final constraints before cutover. Keep any full constraint/index construction outside the pause. G1 should exercise transfers of unique values across keys while chunks land ahead of replay, not only repeated writes to one key.

## Small notes

- **Preserve the prototype’s blocker filter.** [A:263](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:263) omits the observed-token restriction from blocker termination, although the prototype checks it at [node.rs:257](../../snapback4/experiments/postgres-fencing/src/bin/node.rs:257). Without it, a delayed standby can kill a newer owner’s transactions before discovering the changed token. Carry the store and token restrictions into that step; racing-standby qualification remains **UNVERIFIED**.

- **Use SLO-qualified S0 numbers.** [C:226](../../llp/3000.009.001.002-scale-limits-against-postgres-controls.rfc.md:226) associates 150–200/s with the best batch meeting the 100 ms write SLO. The 160/s N=32 cell has p99 **433 ms** ([result:53](../../snapback4/experiments/postgres-commit-path/results-20261019-pass2/model-0.jsonl:53)); the coalesced 192/s cell has p99 **686 ms** ([result:13](../../snapback4/experiments/postgres-commit-path/results-20261019-pass2/model-0-coalesced.jsonl:13)). The rule still fires, so this correction does not change the direction.

- **Fix the raw-Postgres denominator before S1a.** [C:227](../../llp/3000.009.001.002-scale-limits-against-postgres-controls.rfc.md:227)’s “25% … with a 2× margin” needs a specified control connection/concurrency budget. S0’s four-writer and 64-writer results produce very different build thresholds; the comparison should not be selected after sizing (a).
