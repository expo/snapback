# Wave 2, phase A — design one deep application in an assigned archetype

You are designing an application, deeply, so that a *different* model family can
later write it in a candidate backend language and find where that language
breaks. Your design is the fixed product specification for that later step. You
are **not** writing it in any Snapback language, builder, or plan vocabulary —
design it as a product, a data model, and a set of operations in neutral terms
(tables/fields, invariants in plain language, operations as numbered steps or
neutral pseudo-code). Do not read or reference Snapback's authoring surface; if
you know it, forget it for this exercise.

The archetype and the **axis under test** are given in the launcher. The axis is
the thing this archetype exists to stress; design the app so that the axis is
load-bearing in ordinary use, not an edge case. The worked example of the depth
required is LLP 0329.015's booking archetype, whose "trap" was that *active*
versus *historical* membership is a temporal fact a shallow app never surfaces;
find your archetype's equivalent traps and put them in the center of the design.

## Deliverable (≤ 7,000 words; exactly these headings)

### 1. Product contract
What the app is, who uses it, the 10–20 user-visible promises it makes. Include
the concurrency, offline, and multi-device behavior users would expect.

### 2. Invariants, in plain language, before any code
Numbered. Each one a sentence a product manager would sign. Mark the ones that
involve money, secrecy, time, or other people's data.

### 3. The traps, ranked
The 4–6 places where a naive implementation is subtly wrong: temporal (what was
true *when*), concurrency (two actors at once), visibility (who may learn what,
including through counts, ranks, and refusals), authority (who may act on whose
behalf), and merge (two offline devices). For each: the wrong version, why it is
wrong, the observation that exposes it.

### 4. Data model
Tables with fields and types (neutral), keys, the relationships, which fields are
derived from others, which tables are append-only or versioned, which values
are time-bounded.

### 5. The hard operations
10–14 operations (reads and writes), each with: inputs; authorization in plain
language; the exact steps (neutral pseudo-code); what must be atomic; what may
be eventually consistent; what the UI should show optimistically, if anything;
and what concurrent conflict does. Include at least one scheduled/background
operation, one operation that spans an external system if the archetype has
one, and one bulk or periodic operation.

### 6. Scale and cost
Realistic sizes (rows, rates, fan-out) for a successful deployment, and which
operations become expensive at those sizes.

### 7. Where a backend would have to be unusual
The 3–5 requirements you expect no ordinary "queries + mutations over rows"
backend to satisfy cleanly, and what a product could concede instead — with the
boundary at which the concession is a cheat.

### 8. Confidence
What in this design is standard practice versus invented for the exercise.

## Rules
- Modify no repository file. Your final message is the deliverable.
- Be concrete: real field names, real numbers, real step lists. A design another
  model cannot implement without guessing is a failed design.
- Do not soften the axis to make the app easier.
