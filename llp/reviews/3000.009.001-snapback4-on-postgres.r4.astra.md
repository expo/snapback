## Verdict: SOUND WITH MAJOR ISSUES

Revision 3 substantially addresses the earlier fencing, graduation, and sizing findings. Revision 4 does not establish its claimed continuity guarantees: asynchronous checkpoints cannot prevent simultaneous serving, and detecting missing history does not supply the records needed to repair it. Preserving sessions across lossless failover remains a sound objective, but the proposed lossy recovery needs additional durable coordination. This is a read-only review of `c24adea5bc..1c2094a3a9`; implementation and managed-provider behavior remain **UNVERIFIED**.

## Findings

### N1. Conditional checkpoints detect forks after both owners have served — FUNDAMENTAL — child A §5.4.1–§5.4.3

The fork defense relies on an owner stopping when its conditional checkpoint write loses, while checkpointing is asynchronous and serving continues during blob-store outages. See [A:382](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:382) and [A:413](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:413).

Starting from a shared checkpoint, the promoted primary can pass acquisition, advance the checkpoint, and begin serving. The old primary still has a valid owner in its independent database and can acknowledge further writes until its next checkpoint attempt discovers the conflict. If its blob-store connection is unavailable, the specified continuation policy makes that overlap unbounded.

CAS serializes checkpoint updates; it does not serialize database commits or serving authority. Stopping afterward cannot retract acknowledged writes or external effects.

**Recommended direction:** require fencing of the old primary before the successor serves, or specify an external ownership protocol that prevents serving beyond its authority, including during partitions. Keep asynchronous checkpoints as divergence evidence.

### N2. A journaled revocation can disappear without triggering journal recovery — FUNDAMENTAL — child A §5.4.3–§5.4.4

Acquisition checks the checkpoint, but authority-journal replay happens only **after detected loss**. See [A:423](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:423) and [A:455](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:455).

Consider this execution:

1. The checkpoint covers revision `r`.
2. A revocation commits at `r+1`, reaches the synchronous authority journal, and is acknowledged.
3. An asynchronous standby containing only `r` is promoted.
4. Its checkpoint comparison passes.

The revocation need not have appeared in any device’s sync evidence. The revoked token’s holder can make an ordinary authenticated request. Neither specified loss detector fires, so the database can accept the token despite its durable revocation journal.

**Recommended direction:** reconcile the authority journal on every acquisition, before authenticated serving, independently of checkpoint mismatch. Define an authoritative journal position, idempotent replay, and the acquisition policy when that journal cannot be read. This also depends on N1’s exclusion of the former owner.

### N3. The “durable watermark” does not justify discarding acknowledged writes — MAJOR — child A §5.4.3–§5.4.4

The checkpoint stores a position and hash, while devices discard acknowledged writes once that watermark passes them. See [A:413](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:413) and [A:449](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:449).

A device can therefore discard write W after checkpoint advancement, followed by promotion of a replica preceding W. The checkpoint detects the loss, but W’s database record, receipt, and retained device request are all gone. A hash cannot reconstruct them.

There is also an inclusion gap: only sync pages explicitly carry chain hashes. A device whose last sync precedes its acknowledged W can present valid prefix evidence after W is lost. Replacement history can then advance the numeric watermark past W without the specified resume check detecting that loss.

Original operation IDs deduplicate against surviving receipts; they do not prove that a later watermark includes an earlier acknowledgment.

**Recommended direction:** permit disposal only when W is both proven included in the accepted history and recoverable across every permitted promotion. That requires a replication durability guarantee or durable recovery payloads, plus acknowledgment-bound chain evidence or explicit receipt reconciliation.

### N4. Rollback can erase the evidence needed to park an effect — MAJOR — child A §4.3 and §5.4.4

Promotion parks effects recorded as previously claimed, and recovery promises to identify uncertain attempts. See [A:218](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:218) and [A:463](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:463).

At revision `r`, an effect is queued and has never been claimed. At `r+1`, its attempt is recorded and its handler performs an external effect. Promotion then restores `r`. The successor sees an apparently unclaimed effect, with neither an attempt nor a completion. Its checkpoint can still match, and the “ever claimed” parking predicate is false.

The surviving database cannot distinguish this execution from one where the handler never started. “Re-derived” claims and steps cannot recover that missing fact.

**Recommended direction:** make dispatch intent survive every permitted promotion before invoking the handler, through synchronous replication or an external intent journal. Otherwise require provider reconciliation or park a conservative superset of potentially dispatched work; narrow parking cannot rely exclusively on rolled-back attempt records.

### N5. Lossy failover can erase graduation’s winning abort — FUNDAMENTAL — child B §5, interacting with child A §5.4.2

The new `RETURNING` rule correctly resolves the previous abort race within one surviving database history. It still assumes that an acknowledged decision cannot subsequently disappear. That assumption conflicts with newly supported asynchronous failover. See [B:246](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:246) and [A:402](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:402).

An abort can return a row, commit successfully, and reopen SQLite. Promotion can then restore the target’s attempt row to `decision = NULL`. A delayed or retried handoff now satisfies its conditional update and makes Postgres live ([B:235](../../llp/3000.009.001.001-graduating-a-live-store-to-postgres.rfc.md:235)). Both stores become writable.

This is distinct from the repaired zero-row-update bug: both decisions genuinely win, in different database histories.

**Recommended direction:** require graduation decisions to survive every allowed failover before either side acts on them. Use an external durable decision record or explicitly require suitable lossless HA throughout graduation and reversal. Authority-only journaling does not protect these decisions.

## Small notes

- **Checkpoint age is unbounded under the stated outage policy.** Continuing service without checkpoint progress contradicts the one-interval loss and retention bounds at [A:429](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:429) and [A:479](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:479). State actual checkpoint-age and retention behavior.

- **The takeover reread reduces a race; it does not eliminate it.** A renewal can commit between the reread and termination. Narrow the “aborts nothing” assertion at [A:318](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:318). Token bounds protect a successor, but do not atomically protect same-token renewal.

- **Specify the owner-renewal exception to the committer stream.** If “every durable write” includes owner-lease renewals ([A:594](../../llp/3000.009.001.000-the-postgres-store-port.rfc.md:594)), a long batch can delay renewal despite compatible database locks. Preserve an independent renewal path or explain how it progresses while that batch remains open.
