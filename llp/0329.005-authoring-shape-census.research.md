## Summary

[LLP 0329.004](./0329.004-composition-expressiveness-constraint.research.md) asked
whether an analyzable read language can express what real applications need. It
answered from LLP 0120's felt-loss set. This document answers from the whole demo
corpus, by measurement.

Four results. Two relocate the problem, one narrows a claim LLP 0329.004 published,
and one tests the retention-versus-recomputation distinction against a real
acceptance specification.

**Method.** `benchmarks/tools/authoring-shape-census.py`, run from the repository
root against `main` at `84ea92853`. Corpus: 105 backend files under
`demos/*/snapback/*.mjs`, excluding generated code. Constructor counts are exact;
shape classification is regex-based over inline handler bodies and is indicative
rather than exact (see [Confidence](#confidence)).

## Findings

### F1 — The escape hatch is a write-side phenomenon

| Kind | Declarative | JS handler | Escape rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reads | 81 | 43 | **35%** |
| Writes | 38 | 107 | **74%** |

The design conversation this document's parents record — LLP 0120, LLP 0155, and the
three families' sketches in LLP 0329.000 — is almost entirely about the *read*
language. The corpus says two thirds of reads already use the analyzable form, and
three quarters of writes do not.

### F2 — Derived optimistic prediction reaches 19% of mutations

`guide/clients/react.md` states that the default optimistic path requires generated
`local-executable` IR, that "the current table-write projector requires owner-proved
table-complete query coverage; filtered or limited membership gets the same
inspectable pending-only state," and that cross-table plans are withheld with
`SNAPBACK_OPTIMISTIC_CROSS_TABLE_REPLAY_UNPROVEN`.

Declarative **and** single-table: **27 of 143 mutations (19%)**. That is a ceiling
reached before the table-complete coverage test, which 66% of declarative read
queries would fail because they are argument-filtered.

Optimistic prediction is one of the two capabilities LLP 0329.000's three families
agreed require a static plan. It is currently reachable by under a fifth of the
corpus's mutations.

### F3 — Nearly half the read-side escapes use no missing capability

Inline JS read handlers, n = 38:

| Shape | n | % |
|---|---|---|
| A1 single read, no JS logic | 14 | 37% |
| A2 single read + JS filter | 3 | 8% |
| B k independent reads | 12 | 32% |
| C nested / N+1 | 1 | 3% |
| D single read + JS aggregate/sort | 1 | 3% |
| E no db read | 7 | 18% |

**A1 and A2 together are 45%** and use nothing the declarative surface lacks.
Verbatim from the corpus:

```js
handler: async (ctx) => ctx.db.query('profiles').take(100).collect()
handler: async (ctx, args) => {
  const rows = await ctx.db.query('todos').take(1024).collect();
  return rows.filter((row) => row.listId === args.listId);
}
```

The second scans up to 1,024 rows and filters in JavaScript where an index would
serve, and forfeits retention eligibility by being a handler at all
(`guide/sync/offline-and-retention.md`: `defineJsLiveQuery` handlers are never
`rqsEligible`).

**Of the 13 handlers that genuinely need composition, 12 are the single "k
independent bounded reads" shape and one is nested N+1.** This is direct support for
the k-ary bounded-children increment over LLP 0110's one-parent/one-child profile:
one named increment covers 12 of 13 measured composition cases. Aggregation appears
once in this corpus, which does **not** contradict LLP 0120 — its aggregate demand
came from the Relay/Split rerun apps, which this census does not weight specially.

### F4 — The offline milestone specifies rendering and queueing, not recomputation

`benchmarks/briefs/social-messenger.md` §M6, the offline milestone the Clique matrix
scores:

> **Offline reads:** cold-starting the app with the backend unreachable **shows** all
> previously synced chats, messages, and already-viewed media …
>
> **Offline writes:** composing while offline **queues** messages with a visible
> `pending` state; on reconnect they flush **in order** and walk the normal receipt
> states.

Acceptance 1 is "history and previously viewed media render." Acceptance 2 is "send
two messages offline; both show pending."

That is an authorized retained result plus an ordered outbox carrying a pending
marker. In LLP 0329.000's vocabulary it is **`offlineRenderable`, not
`offlineRecomputable`** — the specification asks the client to *display* retained
rows and to *show a queued write as pending*, not to merge a predicted row into a
locally recomputed query result. `guide/clients/react.md` confirms the weaker state
is universally available: missing required reads and `server-only` IR "stay pending
in `optimisticPresentation.queuedOnly` without inventing a row effect," which does
not require IR.

The one genuinely local computation M6 requires is cold **search over synced
content** — a local scan over the retained store, not a re-executed relational plan.

## Consequences

1. **The read-language question is not where the corpus leaves the paved path.**
   F1 relocates it to writes, which no document in this family had examined.
2. **The composition gap is narrow and named.** F3 says one increment covers 12 of 13
   cases, which is the cheapest of the options LLP 0120 enumerated.
3. **Much of the escape is habitual rather than forced.** 45% of read handlers use no
   missing capability. That makes defaulting and discoverability a first-order cause,
   alongside expressiveness.
4. **The capability that forces a restricted language may not be required by the
   shipped product.** If F4 generalizes, `offlineRecomputable` is not on the critical
   path for the offline milestone, and the analyzable core needed to ship it is
   smaller than any of LLP 0329.000's three answers assumed.

This document does **not** establish why writes escape at 74%.
`guide/backend/declarative-plans.md` shows `likePost` writing two tables with a
conditional refusal, and the plan vocabulary includes `guard`, `letBind`,
`insertEach`, `deleteWhere`, and `runtimeNow()` — so multi-table writes (45% of JS
mutations) and read-before-write (76%) are *expressible*. Whether authors escape
because the vocabulary is unfamiliar or because it hits a wall this census did not
find was left open.

> **Answered 2026-08-28 by [LLP 0329.006](./0329.006-why-writes-escape.research.md):
> it is a wall, not unfamiliarity.** Every read a `MutationStep` performs is a
> `"point"` read by `_id` — verified against `crates/snapback-types/src/manifest/types.rs`.
> A declarative mutation therefore cannot check a uniqueness constraint, a membership
> row, or any other non-key invariant, so any write that depends on one must be a JS
> handler. Read plans have no such restriction. That also explains why the escape rate
> climbs with schema size and why widening the plan vocabulary in July did not reverse
> it.

## Confidence

High on the constructor counts and the escape rates: they are exact counts of
balanced-brace constructor bodies, reproducible by re-running the script.

High on F4's reading of the M6 text, which is quoted rather than paraphrased.

Medium on the F3 shape classification. It is regex-based over inline handlers only;
5 of 43 JS reads use the bundled `source`/`exportName` form and are excluded. Two
detector defects were found and corrected during the analysis and are recorded here
so the numbers can be audited: a single-line pattern missed `ctx.db\n.query(`, and an
early pass looked for `ctx.db.insert` in declarative plans, which spell writes as
`insert('table', …)`. Both are fixed in the committed script; earlier figures derived
from them were wrong and are not reported.

Medium-low on generalizing any of this beyond the corpus. Demo applications are
written by agents building benchmark instruments and by the team, several to exercise
one feature. They are not a random sample of applications.

F4 reads one milestone of one instrument. It shows that *this* offline product does
not require local recomputation. It does not show that no product does — a
Linear-style application in which an offline edit must re-sort or re-filter a list
would.

## Open questions

1. **Why do 74% of writes escape,** given that the plan vocabulary expresses the two
   dominant JS-mutation shapes? Distinguishing "unfamiliar" from "hits a wall" is the
   highest-value follow-up in this family.
2. **Does F4 generalize?** For each screen in the Clique instrument that shows a
   queued write, is the product requirement a pending badge or a merged, recomputed
   list? That answer sizes the analyzable language.
3. **Is the read-side habit cheap to reverse?** If 45% of handlers need no missing
   capability, does a defaulting or diagnostic change move them, and does that show up
   as retention eligibility the corpus is currently forfeiting silently?
