## Summary

A derived fact in a Snapback 4 application should be declared and kept by the
engine. Today the application often keeps it by hand, and the rules then have
to verify it. The engine keeps one derivation shape: a count or sum grouped by
the source's own columns (LLP 3000.012 §2.4). Everything else is application
code: the Following feed, rankings, attribution copies. Those are recurring
charges in the weakest categories (LLP 3000.012 §2.2).

This RFC proposes one admission rule with five conditions, three forms and one
shared substrate.

- **The admission rule.** The engine writes a derivation when five things
  hold:
  - its inputs are transaction-visible rows, applied as actual transitions;
  - every operation that touches it has bounded work;
  - its disclosure is bounded and stated: the aggregate to its audience, and
    recorded facts to the source's audience;
  - its output is ordinary rows with a declared audience;
  - it declares a lifecycle policy.

  This keeps §21.2's reason, bounded work in the writing transaction. It
  narrows §21.7's exclusion of join views to two admitted cases: attribution
  recorded through immutable references to public targets, and the Following
  recipe's capped timeline.
- **F1, maintained columns.** LLP 3000.007.001 as accepted. It follows an
  explicit ruling on MC1's gate and generation discovery before
  materialization (§5).
- **F2, engine-recorded attribution.** For example,
  `count likes by postId.authorId`. When a source row is inserted, the engine
  resolves the reference and records the target's facts on it, so
  incremental maintenance, expiry and rebuild all read the same recorded
  value. F2 does not remove the copy. It moves the copy's writer from the
  application to the engine, and that is what removes the verifying rule, the
  copy writes and the backfill.
- **F3, timelines.** The Following recipe's contract as a declaration. Its
  historical domain, permanent promotion, capacity refusals, event identity and
  deletion are stated rather than implied. The first implementation relocates
  the recipe into engine-written tables within its supported shape.
- **One substrate.** One executor, one transaction boundary, one catalog of
  derived declarations and one sync protocol. Each form has an explicit
  lifecycle policy.

In Clique this deletes:

- the recipe's application-maintained projection tables and helpers;
- the three copied columns on `likes`, their verification clauses and the copy
  writes;
- `posts.replyAuthor` and the backfill job;
- with F1, the ranking's copied time and its zero-like fill.

It adds no query planner, no general view maintenance, no ceiling and no second
language.

Revision 1 was reviewed by Astra at max effort and found NOT READY, and so was
revision 2 in a delta review. This revision answers both (§12). The §11
questions were decided under the author's delegation, as LLP 3000.007 §14
rulings 45–51. F2 proceeds to implementation on this revision's contract,
with two-family blind review of the implementation. F3's focused review found
it READY WITH CHANGES. Revision 4 answers that review, so F3's lane can start
from it.

## 1. The admission rule

LLP 3000.007 §21.3 P5 names three writer classes: principals through rules;
the engine through a declaration; and the system through a job (with rules
`deny`). A fact computed from other rows belongs to the engine class when all
five conditions hold.

1. **Its inputs are transaction-visible rows.** That means committed rows plus
   the writing mutation's own earlier writes: a like inserted after its post,
   in one mutation, resolves that post. It uses no `now`, `viewer` or helper,
   as maintain predicates do today. F2's recorded facts become values of the
   source row at contribution time. Derivations follow actual transitions in
   order: an unfollow followed by a refollow is two transitions, never one
   collapsed final state.
2. **Every operation that touches it has bounded work.** That covers:
   - source insert, update and delete;
   - edge insert and delete;
   - target deletion;
   - cleanup;
   - adoption.

   Each form publishes an operation matrix of rows, index entries and bytes.
   Declared caps and the meter enforce it, and `check --cost` prints it. A cap
   per key bounds one key's rows; it does not by itself bound a lifecycle
   operation, so the matrix states each operation separately.
3. **Its disclosure is bounded and stated.** It discloses:
   - the aggregate, to its declared read audience, as every maintain does
     today (F1's counts over private sources included, per LLP 3000.007.001
     §3.8);
   - recorded facts, to the source row's audience, as historical facts that
     were readable (public) when recorded, governed by the source's retention;
   - nothing else.

   No new refusal may reveal whether a row the writer cannot read exists.
   First scopes:
   - public targets, for F2;
   - the recipe's public shape, for F3.
4. **Its output is ordinary rows** (or, for F1, a field) **with a declared read
   audience.** No principal may write the output; the maintain-target refusal
   extends to every engine-written table.
5. **It declares a lifecycle policy:** activation, rebuild, device behavior,
   recorded provenance and internal dependencies (§4).

A derivation that fails any condition stays in the principal or system class.
A diagnostic names the condition that failed.

## 3. The forms

Spellings are illustrative. None of the blocks below is a compiling example.

### 3.0 Aggregate target identity and migration

Every ordinary maintained aggregate row has one internal identity function:
64 lowercase hexadecimal characters from SHA-256 over the domain
`snapback4-maintained-id/sha256-v1` and the typed canonical group-key vector.
The checker therefore admits the largest legal group key against a fixed-size
identity, and synthetic check rows, incremental writes, point reads and
adoption rebuilds all call the same function. The group key remains the public
address; programs do not read or supply this internal id.

The intrinsic `byId` order of aggregate rows is consequently opaque digest
order. It is stable for one identity version but has no relationship to the
lexical or numeric order of group fields, and migration may change it. A query
whose result needs group-key order declares that order explicitly (for example,
`first 50 by .emoji`) rather than relying on an unqualified aggregate scan.

The SQLite metadata and evaluation-PostgreSQL backend marker
`maintained_id_version = sha256-v1` bind persisted aggregate targets to that
function. Opening or adopting either store without the marker — including a
0.4.28 store whose ids were the unbounded canonical JSON of the group key —
atomically clears and rebuilds every ordinary aggregate target from its source
rows, writes the marker, and publishes a new semantic generation. A failure
rolls back old rows, rebuilt rows, marker and generation together. Adoption
advice counts the SQLite rebuild even when schema/program bytes are unchanged;
the evaluation adapter performs the corresponding rebuild under its opening
writer/head fence. Before clearing targets, adoption derives each target's
legacy-id-to-digest map to a fixed point over typed reference dependencies,
then rewrites every surviving typed reference through those maps. Thus an
aggregate grouped by a reference to another aggregate uses that referenced
aggregate's new digest when deriving its own digest, and a table that refers
to either rebuilt target does not dangle. A legacy id with no matching stored
target stays opaque, preserving the existing dangling-reference contract.
Reference rewrites, index updates, target rebuilds, marker and generation share
the same transaction. The fixed-point bound counts stored aggregate identities,
not aggregate declarations, because one target can hold a legal acyclic chain
of references to other rows in that same target. SQLite captures newly recorded
path values while predecessor targets still exist, then derives and applies all
legacy maps before any recorded-path rebuild can remove those targets. Schemas
with ordinary maintains require device capability
`maintained-id-sha256-v1`; an older device is refused at the handshake before
it can compute a legacy id and silently read a digest-addressed aggregate as
zero.

### 3.1 F1: maintained columns

F1 is LLP 3000.007.001, unchanged. Its value here is ranking. With `likeCount`
on the post and ruling 29's hour index, Trending becomes one merge over 25 hour
lanes (§21.3 P3):

- zero-like posts are included;
- ties break by post time;
- there is no copied time and no fill.

F1 composes with F3 on the same posts table: `likeCount` and the hour index
sit on the posts a Following timeline maps. A timeline's **owned** tables are
never a column's parent or source. The engine writes them only on the
timeline's scoped transition path, and a mirror there would bypass it. Its
**mapped** tables are ordinary parents and sources, for three reasons:
- the mirror is not a source write, so it never enters a timeline
  transition;
- delivery copies ids, author and time, never parent fields;
- a mapped source's own write runs its maintain, then its transition, like
  any maintain over a mapped table.

A differential test shows it. The same operations on the language card's
timeline, with and without counts on its mapped cards, leave every owned row
and home page identical. The operations are follows, posts, a delivered
repost, votes, removal, unvote and unfollow.

Its cost is recorded in LLP 3000.007.002 §2: each contribution re-sends the
parent row. On a 4,000-byte parent that is 4,323 wire bytes per like, against
329 for a separate synced maintain. F2 keeps aggregates in their own rows. It
is the better form for a hot parent's counter until the stream coalesces or
patches fields.

### 3.2 F2: engine-recorded attribution

```text
maintain receivedLikes: count likes by postId.authorId
  read <- allow
maintain dailyLikes: count likes by postId.authorId, day(.at)
  read <- allow
maintain receivedReplies: count posts where .parentId != null by parentId.authorId
  read <- allow
```

**The contract: attribution is a contribution-time fact.**

- When a source row is inserted, the engine resolves each reference path with
  one point read. It records the target facts the derivation reads, both
  group-key paths and predicate paths, in engine-written fields on the source
  row.
- Programs, seeds, imports and native jobs cannot write those fields.
- Every consumer reads the recorded fields, not the target:
  - incremental deltas;
  - inverse deltas on delete;
  - live recomputation before an expiry sweep, which today recomputes from the
    source row (`engine/read.rs`);
  - rebuild on adoption, which today deletes the aggregate and folds source
    rows again (`engine/maintains.rs:71`).

  Because the key is a source-row value, the existing maintain machinery
  computes it from the source row unchanged. Incremental maintenance and
  rebuild therefore agree by construction. This is the counterexample Astra's
  review raised against revision 1, which recorded nothing.

**Consequences, stated as semantics.**

- Deleting a target neither moves nor removes a contribution.
- Recreating a target with the same id does not re-attribute earlier
  contributions. Today's hand copies behave the same way.
- An existing index does not substitute for recorded facts. The contribution
  index expiring maintains use today recomputes membership and keys from the
  source row (`maintain.rs:113`); it records no foreign facts. The recorded
  fields are what make that recomputation correct for F2.

**First scope.**

| Aspect | Rule |
|---|---|
| Targets | Public: the target table's read rule is `public` or `allow` |
| Depth | One hop |
| The source's reference column | Must be `immutable`, so an update never re-resolves; `likes.postId` in calibration #16 already is |
| The target columns read | Must be `immutable`, so a live target's recorded value equals its current one |

Private targets, mutable paths and multiple hops need their own disclosure and
lifecycle analysis, and are out of scope.

**Unresolved references record nulls; they do not refuse.** Typed references
do not imply existence (`engine/validate.rs:102`). A refusal would reveal
whether a row exists, and LLP 3000.007.001 §5 already rejected missing-parent
refusals for that reason and for forward references, seeding order and unheld
parents. A dangling or not-yet-created target therefore records null facts and
contributes to the null group. `doctor` counts null-attributed contributions
per derivation. An application that needs the target keeps its own
`require exists`. The source's independent authorization stays; `likes` keeps
`insert <- .principal = viewer`.

**Recorded paths are history, and capture is separate from rebuild.**

- **A recorded field is identified by its path.** Every maintain on the source
  table that uses the path shares the field.
- **Once written, a recorded value is never re-resolved or overwritten.** That
  holds for as long as any maintain uses its path, across every generation.
  Rebuilding an aggregate, including one whose predicate or keys changed over
  existing paths, reads the recorded values. It never reads the targets. This is
  what makes incremental maintenance and rebuild agree.
- **Adding a path to a populated table is an activation-time capture.** It
  happens once, as a migration step separate from, and before, the aggregate
  rebuild. Each existing row's value is resolved against the live target at
  activation, and is null when the target is absent or expired.

  This is exactly what a hand-written backfill job does today, and the
  semantics are stated as such. For rows that predate the path, the recorded
  fact is the target's value at activation, not at contribution. If a target id
  was deleted and reused before activation, the value is the reusing row's. It
  is never guessed. `doctor` reports how many rows each capture resolved to
  null.
- **Development removal drops the path; release removal retains it dormant.**
  Development re-adding a removed path is a new activation-time capture.
  Release activation follows [LLP 3000.000.000](./3000.000.000-retained-storage-and-hot-release.rfc.md): restoring a dormant recorded field preserves its physical historical value, including null. Only rows without historical evidence receive a new activation-time capture; aggregate rebuilds consume the preserved values. Explicit prune removes dormant history.

**Resolution is live.** The engine resolves against rows that are live under
the read path's own absence rules. An expired target, which is logically absent
before its sweep (`engine/read.rs`), resolves to null exactly as a deleted one
does.

**Device prediction.** A device resolves a predicted insert only against a
target row that is live **and** covered by its current partition. A
retained-only image does not count: public tables may use subset delivery with
retained history, and a retained image can outlive coverage or miss a
deletion or recreation. A target the device does not hold, or holds only as
retained history, queues the mutation without a predicted row, which is the
existing unpredicted-queue class. The server's resolution is authoritative, and
a prediction is replaced by the server's image when it arrives.
`check --residency` lists the implicit dependency on the target table.

**Historical disclosure.** Recorded fields are part of the source row. They
travel to the source's audience, including readers who arrive after the target
was deleted or expired, and they follow the source's retention policy.
`retain delivered-history`, if present, retains them with the row. With public
targets, each recorded fact was public when recorded. That is the disclosure
condition 3 of §1 names, and nothing more.

**Field ownership and enforcement.**

- **The schema marks each recorded field as engine-recorded.** That
  per-field metadata sits beside the maintain-target refusal, which today
  protects only whole maintain tables (`engine/write.rs:301`).
- **The write guard refuses any program write that names a recorded field.**
  That covers insert, update and upsert payloads, a no-op write that repeats
  the stored value, native-job writes, and system callers such as jobs.
- **`Engine::load` has two modes** (`engine/mod.rs:968`), because it bypasses
  the mutation path and writes validated rows directly. LLP 3000.007.001 §3.2
  draws the same distinction for maintained columns.
  - **Trusted persistence restoration**, including device reopen and stream
    page application, keeps stored recorded values as they are.
  - **Untrusted fixture and seed import** refuses supplied recorded values. It
    captures them at import, against the imported targets, with
    activation-time semantics.

**Nullability.**

- A recorded field's type is the target column's type made optional; null
  means unresolved.
- A calendar key over a null recorded time (`hour(postId.at)`) yields the null
  group. Today's `CalendarKey` falls back to zero on a missing value
  (`maintain.rs`); F2 must not.
- Equality predicates on paths follow the value model's null semantics.
  Ordered comparisons on a path are refused at `check`, as ordered comparisons
  on optional columns already are.

**Activation publishes no row events.** Stamping recorded fields into existing
rows, and rebuilding the aggregates that read them, follows LLP 3000.007.001
§3.11's activation policy:

- it runs as an event-free generation barrier;
- devices holding affected tables reacquire by snapshot, which is paged and not
  commit-bound;
- it appends no commit whose size scales with the table.

**Cost.** Each contributing insert costs one point read per recorded path.
Recorded bytes per source row are about what today's hand copies cost, and are
measured when F2 lands.

**The first acceptance test is equivalence.** Incremental maintenance and a
rebuild must agree on both stores after each of these:

- target deletion;
- target recreation with the same id;
- source expiry before the sweep;
- a predicate change;
- adoption over dangling references.

Without F1, Trending under F2 is
`count likes where postId.parentId = null by hour(postId.at), postId.at, postId`.
The copies and their verification clauses go; the zero-like fill stays.

### 3.3 F3: the Following recipe's contract as a declaration

A timeline's **answer contract** is separate from its **delivery strategy**.
The contract says what Following contains. Push, pull, or a mix is how the
engine serves it, and can change without changing the contract. The first
scope preserves the recipe's contract exactly, as the
[feeds card](../snapback4/crates/snapback4-lang/src/guide/feeds.md) states it
today.

**The answer contract.**

- **Content.** A reader's timeline holds the reader's own events and the
  events of followed authors.
  - An event is an original or a repost, with one source event each.
  - A repost carries the original post's id and author, with the reposter as
    actor.
  - Repeating a repost inserts nothing a second time.
- **Historical domain.** Following an author examines the newest 865 of that
  author's source events and copies the pushed ones among them. It does not
  copy the newest 865 pushed events: with 900 older pushed events and 100
  newer shared ones, a later follow copies 765 (the feeds card's `visited`
  advances before the `shared` test). A shared (promoted) author's events are
  read directly from the public broadcast projection. This is not an unlimited
  archive, and a merge over followed authors' posts would promise more.
- **Identity and order.**
  - Every event has its own id: an original, or a repost, whose id is distinct
    from its original's.
  - Event identities are fresh. An original's event is published once: its
    card is retained and cannot be deleted, and its removal flag is one-way.
    A repost's event has a fresh id, so successive reposts are distinct
    events.
  - Re-creating a removed event id is refused (`FEED_EVENT_REMOVED`) only while
    its tombstone exists. The purge job deletes tombstones, so the guarantee
    rests on the retained cards and the one-way flag. Tombstones are not kept
    forever, because that would change the recipe's cleanup and capacity
    contract.
  - Removing an original leaves other actors' reposts of it standing.
  - Pages run newest first, by time, then by event id. Tombstoned events are
    filtered before a page is emitted.
  - A meter cut can end a page incomplete without a continuation (the feeds
    card).
- **Promotion.** Promotion is permanent, and atomic with the follow that
  triggers it (the 51st follower). It installs a pull route for every
  follower, including the triggering reader.
  - `FEED_PULL_CAP` refuses when the caller's own 32 shared-author slots are
    full.
  - `FEED_PROMOTION_PULL_CAP` refuses the whole follow when any other
    follower's slots are full. That can leave an author at 50 followers
    indefinitely.
- **Unfollow.** Removes that author's private copies and pull route in the
  same transaction.
- **Removal.** The application's posts cannot be deleted in the supported
  shape: removing an original keeps its card for independent reposts. So the
  declaration names a **removal signal**:
  - an original is removed by setting a declared boolean column on the post to
    true, and that column may only go from false to true;
  - a repost is removed by deleting the application's repost row, which the
    owner may already do.

  The flag's transitions:
  - it is false on insert, and an initially true value is refused in the first
    scope;
  - only false → true removes;
  - a repeated removal, or an unchanged flag, does no removal work;
  - a new repost requires a live original event, as the bridge requires, while
    an existing repost survives its original's removal.

  Either removal writes a public tombstone with the event's id, in the same
  transaction. Following hides the event at once. A system job runs every
  minute and purges private copies, at most 1,001 per event per chunk. Removal
  never probes or deletes another reader's private inbox rows in the author's
  transaction; cleanup is deferred, as in the recipe.
- **Caps.** Reaching any cap refuses the write atomically.

  | Limit | Value |
  |---|---:|
  | Follows per account | 1,000 |
  | Followers per author | 1,000 |
  | Source events per actor | 1,000 |
  | Private entries per reader | 50,000 |
  | Private entries per reader and actor | 1,000 |
  | Private copies per event | 1,001 |
  | Pulled authors per reader | 32 |
  | Public broadcast entries | 32,000 |
  | Pending tombstones | 32,000 (1,000 per actor) |
  | Global: authors | 1,000 |
  | Global: edges | 100,000 |
  | Global: pull routes | 32,000 |
  | Global: online count rows | 32,000 |
  | Global: source references | 60,000 |

  The per-reader-and-actor and per-event caps are what bound unfollow and
  cleanup. The source-event cap alone does not bound copies awaiting purge.

- **Prediction.** Posting, reposting and removal predict when the required rows
  are held. Follow and unfollow queue without predicted rows, because slot
  accounting is server state. Following reads the reader's installed lanes on
  the device.
- **Disclosure.**
  - Public: the follow graph, authors' delivery modes, event metadata and slot
    counts.
  - Readable only by their reader: private pull routes.

**The supported shape is the recipe's.** It has four application tables:
profiles, posts, reposts and follows. Each is explicitly `public`, fully
`sync public`, and has no horizon, expiry or maintained definition. Ownership
and immutability rules are as the card lists them:

- inserts require owner equals `viewer`;
- the mapped identity and time columns are immutable;
- follows and reposts deny updates and delete by owner;
- post deletion is denied, because removal keeps cards for independent
  reposts.

Broader combinations need their own proof. These include private edges, an
expiring source or edge, horizons, and mutable ordering or selection fields.

```text
maintain following: timeline
  authors profiles by principal
  events posts by authorId, at removed by removed; reposts by actorId, at
  edges follows by followerId, followedId

query home(c: cursor ?):
  return following[viewer] after c last 20 postCard
```

**The timeline's state is durable and engine-owned; it is not rebuildable.**
Three kinds of history cannot be recomputed from the four application tables:

- each event's delivery mode at publication (pushed or shared);
- each author's permanent promotion, which survives follower counts falling;
- tombstones.

They live in engine-owned tables, the recipe's own, now engine-written:

- the event log, with each event's delivery mode;
- author modes;
- pull routes and slot counts;
- tombstones;
- private inboxes;
- the broadcast projection.

These rows are maintained incrementally and never rebuilt. A timeline's
lifecycle policy is **no rebuild**. Redefining a declared timeline on a
populated store is refused in the first scope.

- **Adoption with an unchanged declaration preserves** all of this state.
- **Trusted persistence restoration** (`Engine::load`, reopen) preserves the
  complete durable state without rerunning publication.
- **Untrusted fixture import** of the four application tables cannot restore F3
  history. It refuses. Replaying defined bounded transitions instead would be
  a later extension.

**The mechanism.**

- **What runs.** The engine runs the recipe's logic on each actual transition
  of the mapped source, removal and edge tables, inside the writing mutation's
  transaction and under that operation's meter. That covers an event insert, a
  removal-flag change, a repost delete, a follow and an unfollow.
- **Authority.** The application's writes still pass their own rules and
  constraints. The engine's internal authority covers only the declaration's
  owned tables.
- **A trusted internal write path, not the tail.** Its writes keep the caps,
  uniqueness, identity and immutability checks that the ordinary write path
  performs. `row_changed` is only the metering and partition tail, and
  `apply_aggregate` writes storage directly; neither is enough by itself. The
  recipe's internal count over its pull routes is an explicit internal
  dependency, because `Origin::Maintained` writes do not run downstream
  maintains.
- **Disclosure stays the recipe's:**
  - private inboxes and pull routes are readable only by their reader;
  - event metadata, author modes, tombstones and broadcasts are public;
  - slot counts are public and online-only;
  - the internal totals are unreadable.

  Engine access to foreign rows grants no application read authority. Fresh
  copies are delivered unconditionally; posting never probes another reader's
  private inbox.
- **Authors are registered eagerly, where the recipe registers them.** Every
  profile insert after activation registers its author's mode row in the same
  mutation, which is exactly where the recipe's `register` call sits.
  Activation registers each profile that already exists, bounded by the
  1,000-author cap, in its one activation commit. Activation therefore needs
  empty event and edge tables, not empty profiles.

  Lazy registration was tried and refused. Lane F3a's second counterexample
  showed that moving the registration write from profile creation into the
  first post makes a posting mutation at the 1,024-write ceiling refuse under
  F3 when the recipe admits it.

**Device execution.** The timeline logic also runs in the device interpreter's
provisional transaction.

- **Posting, reposting and removal predict their derived rows when their inputs
  are held.** Inputs include the author's mode and the reader's follow edges.
  Foreign output rows, such as another reader's inbox copy, need not be held
  already. The existing both-store witness expects the application post, the
  source event, the author's own inbox copy and a follower's inbox copy for a
  one-follower post.
- **Follow and unfollow queue the whole mutation without predicted rows,**
  including its application edge, because slot accounting reads online-only
  counts. The descriptor adds these implicit dependencies to execution
  classification, and the runtime enforces them.
- **Event-id allocation, replay, rollback and settlement** stay compatible with
  the recipe's, on both device stores.

**Activation is first-scoped to empty tables.** Declaring a timeline activates
only while its mapped event and edge tables are empty. Otherwise adoption
refuses, with a rewrite: declare it before data exists; in `dev`, start a fresh
store. Activation over populated data is deferred, because it needs a write
barrier: the feeds card's existing adoption instructions pause publishing,
removals and follows during installation, and `complete: false` does not
prevent a racing backfill or a resurrection. That includes migration from an
installed recipe.

**The operation matrix.** The engine owns every row in it.

| Operation | Engine work, bounded by the contract | Refusals |
|---|---|---|
| Event insert, author not promoted | One source event; up to 50 follower copies plus the author's own copy | Caps |
| Event insert, author promoted | One source event; one broadcast row, plus an own-inbox copy when the author is saturated | Caps |
| Event removal (removal flag set, or repost row deleted) | A tombstone insert; source-event and broadcast deletes; copies purged later | The tombstone caps (32,000 in total, 1,000 per actor) |
| Event update | The mapped identity and time columns are immutable; the removal flag only goes from false to true | Existing immutability; reversing the removal flag |
| Edge insert (follow) | Reads up to 1,000 of the author's source rows; the newest 865 are the membership window, and the pushed events among them are copied. A promoting follow's writes fit the **159-write reserved allowance**, which is the total: three writes per pull route plus three non-route writes, including the application's own edge. The recipe also writes a second, duplicate edge into its own follow table; F3 does not, because the engine reads the mapped application follows directly. A maximum-backfill promotion therefore uses 1,023 writes under F3, where the recipe uses exactly 1,024. The one write of headroom is the only accounting difference. Anything over the limit is an atomic refusal, never a shortened history | `FEED_PULL_CAP`, `FEED_PROMOTION_PULL_CAP`, caps, `E_BOUND` |
| Edge update | Refused: follows deny updates | Existing rule |
| Edge delete (unfollow) | Up to 1,000 private copies of that actor (the per-reader-and-actor cap), plus route and count updates | `FEED_PULL_COUNT_MISSING` |
| Cleanup job | Up to 1,001 copies and one tombstone per chunk (the per-event cap) | Parks on refusal |
| Activation | Only over empty mapped tables in the first scope | Refused otherwise, with a rewrite |

Two more bounds apply.

- **Composition with the application's writes.** The engine's writes join the
  application's own in the same mutation, under the mutation write ceiling of
  1,024 rows. `check --cost` reports the combined worst case for each
  operation.
- **Index and byte bounds.** For each row the matrix lists, the implementing
  lane states its index entries and bytes, and measures them before landing.

**The first implementation is a relocation.**

- The declaration expands into the recipe's tables as engine-written tables,
  with the recipe's caps and algorithm.
- The recipe's internal count over its routes needs an explicit internal
  dependency path, or a directly maintained fact. The reason is that
  `Origin::Maintained` writes skip downstream maintains
  (`engine/write.rs:497`), so a relocated `feedPulls` would not update
  `feedPullTotals` on its own.
- The oracle is the recipe's existing tests: the simulator's
  `following_candidates_at_scale`, the device tests on both stores, and the
  220-post lifecycle case.

**Deliverability (revision 7).** Whole commits are never split
(`partition.rs:633`). But the stream re-reads **current** row images into a
historical commit (`partition.rs:701–711`), so a commit that fits when it is
admitted can outgrow the ceiling later.

Lane F3b drove this on the real engine, the HTTP encoder and both device
stores:

- one mutation published a post and inserted 1,021 small notes: 1,024 rows,
  146,471 bytes on the stream;
- 1,021 later mutations each grew one note to its declared maximum, and each
  of those pages fit;
- a client still behind the first commit then asked for it at `limit: 1` and
  got 18,524,473 bytes, over the 16,777,216-byte ceiling.

This is a property of every commit, not of F3, and no write-time check can
see later growth. The recovery already exists. At limit 1, an oversized
stream reply carries `snapshot: true`, and the client restarts acquisition by
snapshot, where pages are bounded by rows, not commits (MC0's typed
dispatcher). So:

- **Each form bounds its own derived rows.** F3's owned rows have fixed,
  small widths (the lane's measured table, at most 990 wire bytes a row).
  `check --cost` reports the bytes each operation contributes, and a test
  asserts the largest derived contribution to a commit, a maximum-backfill
  promotion, against the ceiling with its margin.
- **A lagging client recovers.** A regression test built from F3b's
  counterexample drives the SDK client, on the WASM and the native SQLite
  device, behind a timeline commit that has outgrown the ceiling. The client
  must reach the server's Following page through the snapshot restart, with
  pending writes intact.
- **No write-time enforcement is added.** It cannot see later growth, and it
  would refuse commits that are deliverable today.

**Owned-row identity (revision 7).** Lane F3a's review found that owned rows
took ids from the caller's `newIds` queue. A caller could then steer an owned
private row onto an id, and a later collision refusal, or its absence,
revealed whether another reader's private inbox row existed. Every owned row
now takes a digest identity: SHA-256 over a fixed domain tag, the timeline's
name, the owned table and the row's natural key, encoded in at most 64
characters. Natural-key encoding alone would exceed the 144-byte identity
limit for a legal 135-byte post id. A device computes the same ids, so it can
predict owned rows without caller ids. Event ids are unchanged: they are
public, and the oracle compares them exactly.

The delta review then found that a **repost** could take a removed event's
id from the caller. Its delivery targets the digest of `[reader, event]`,
which collides with a private copy still awaiting purge. So every new event,
original or repost, refuses with `FEED_EVENT_REMOVED` while a tombstone
exists for its id. That is the identity rule above, now applied to reposts
too. It is sufficient for three reasons:

- tombstones are public;
- the purge job deletes an event's copies together with its tombstone, and
  the per-event cap keeps that to one chunk;
- a live event's id collides first on its public event row.

The recipe admits such a reuse, because its copies take fresh caller ids. The
recipe-trace oracle therefore has a **second documented exception**: F3
refuses a caller-chosen event id equal to a tombstoned event's id. It is
tested as its own case, never as a general relaxation.

**Delivery alternatives.** Keys-only all-pull serving is a measured
alternative strategy under the same contract. §21.3 P1 recorded it at about
2.5 ms absolute per 500-lane page, and 2.5–3.1× metered, against the brief's
3× check. Choosing it is a product judgment about latency, acquisition bytes
and complexity. It qualifies only if it preserves the same membership history:
the window examined at follow time, and permanent promotion. It is not
automatically an implementation of this contract.

### 3.4 Not proposed

- **Live attribution,** where a contribution follows its current target. It
  needs a fan-out on every target change or deletion, and rebuild would diverge
  from incremental maintenance.
- **Cascading a target's deletion into its contributions.** The work is
  unbounded without a cap on referencing rows.
- **Aggregates over a fan-out,** such as a count per reader over a timeline.
- **Arbitrary join views and multi-hop paths.**
- **A "latest row per group" column,** such as `conversations.lastAt`. The
  ordered read `messages[conversationId] last 1 by byChatTime` answers it.
- **Symmetric audiences for pair rows,** such as `dmPairs`. That is a
  union-audience question (`snapback4/NOT-DOING.md`).

## 4. One substrate, with per-form lifecycle policies

Today's derived writes go through `apply_aggregate`
(`crates/snapback4-core/src/engine/maintains.rs`) and
`row_changed(…, Origin::Maintained)`. The partition log, horizons and the
device treat their output as rows. That shared row-change tail is useful, but
it is not yet a generic derivation executor. `Origin::Maintained`
deliberately skips downstream maintains and asset placement, and the
engine-owned-table refusal recognizes maintain targets specifically
(`engine/write.rs:301`, `:497`).

The proposal:

- **Shared: one executor, one transaction boundary, one catalog of derived
  declarations and one sync protocol.**
- **Each declaration carries a descriptor** that records:
  - the sources and targets it reads;
  - the facts it records (F2), or the rows it owns (F1's private aggregate and
    mirror; F3's tables);
  - its internal dependencies: which engine-owned rows feed which;
  - its operation matrix and its audience;
  - its lifecycle policy: activation (atomic rebuild, an event-free barrier,
    or a chunked job), device behavior (derive, predict, or queue), and
    recorded provenance.
- **Commands read from the descriptor.** `check --cost` prints the operation
  matrix; `check --residency` prints the audience and device behavior;
  `doctor` reports activation progress and null attributions; adoption follows
  the lifecycle policy.

Per-form policies are expected to differ. F1 already chose no device derivation
in its first build, a private aggregate with a mirror, and an event-free
activation. Needing another policy hook is not evidence that the core must be
restructured.

## 5. MC1's gate and delivery safety

MC1 recorded adoption passing 2/2 and the no-oversize clause passing 1/2
([record](../benchmarks/runs/2026-09-19-snapback4-column-activation/README.md)).
MC0's adopt-before-shrink recovery does not pass that recorded gate. Accepting
the recovery would be a prospective revision of the gate, and that ruling is
the author's.

The recommendation has two parts:

- **Commission request-side generation discovery before materialization.**
  This fixes the stale-generation case MC1 met.
- **Separately, bound and test the largest current-generation commit each
  derived form can produce.** Halving a request cannot split a whole commit.
  Revision 7 narrows this to each form's **own** derived rows. A commit can
  outgrow the ceiling after admission, because the stream re-reads current
  images. A lagging client recovers through the snapshot restart at limit 1
  (§3.3, deliverability).
  - **F1:** not activation, which deliberately emits no partition row events
    (LLP 3000.007.001 §3.11). It is an ordinary write whose source changes and
    whole-parent mirror images compose within one mutation.
  - **F2:** activation is event-free (§3.2). An ordinary insert grows only by
    its recorded fields.
  - **F3:** a promoting follow with its backfill and the application's own
    writes.
- **Clients that send no generation keep today's behavior,** including MC0's
  recovery.

## 9. Risks

- **The engine owns more code.** The recipe's logic moves to the core owner
  (LLP 3000 decision 4), and gains blind review where it touches delivery.
- **Timelines are shaped by one domain.** A second application should validate
  the form without Clique's public-graph assumptions before it widens.
  Workroom or Interview could do that.
- **Hot parents under F1** (§3.1).
- **Activation cost is unmeasured.** MC1's 51.255 s was ordinary activation
  after a separate stopped-store backfill. It says nothing about F1's evaluator
  or mirror, F2's resolution, or F3's chunked activation.
- **Recorded attribution is a durable copy.** It needs storage and a repair
  story, and it travels with the source row.
- **The score may not move.** Quarry's learning cost is charged in every run
  (LLP 3000.012 §2.2). The counters are the claim.
