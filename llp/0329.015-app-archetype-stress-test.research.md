## Summary

[LLP 0329.008](./0329.008-coterie-plan-expressibility.research.md) derived a ladder
(A–D: one primitive at four `take` values) from **one** application archetype, social
messaging. [LLP 0329.011](./0329.011-bound-read-security-contract.research.md)
proposed a security contract for it and noted the contract had only been tested
against apps with no protected tables.

Four new applications were designed to falsify both, deliberately chosen to hit axes
the corpus had never exercised, and deliberately required to be *deep* — the brief
used `coterie`'s active-versus-historical membership as the worked example of a
temporal trap that a shallow app would never surface.

**All four independently conclude the model does not survive.** The refutations
converge, which is the useful part.

| Archetype | Family | Axis under test | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resource booking | Claude Opus | interval/range predicates, cancellation, waitlists | 27% of mutations become plans |
| Document workspace | Claude Opus | recursive ACL, subtree moves, share links | **0 of 12** operations become plans |
| Sealed-bid auction | codex `gpt-5.6-sol` | time-gated visibility, ranking, settlement | does not survive as an A–D claim |
| Double-entry ledger | `grok-4.6` | sums, immutability, retroactive edits | "mostly JS after rungs A–D" |

## Relationship to the concurrent series (LLP 0329.012–0329.014)

A second session worked the same question in parallel and landed
[LLP 0329.012](./0329.012-bound-carrying-algebra-as-the-only-language.research.md),
[0329.013](./0329.013-one-read-language-proposals.research.md), and the panel
synthesis [0329.014](./0329.014-bounded-data-programs-panel-synthesis.research.md).
Neither series saw the other while running.

They are **not in conflict, and the relationship is worth stating** because a reader
could mistake one for a refutation of the other.

- LLP 0329.013's central claim is that *"expressiveness was never lost to
  restriction. It was lost to a registry"* — the analyzable surface was built one
  admitted shape at a time (two-source unique join, one-parent-one-child,
  snapshot-batch tuple), and the answer is a general bound-carrying algebra rather
  than more admitted shapes.
- This document tests the **A–D ladder** of LLP 0329.008, which is itself a proposal
  for *more admitted shapes*.

So the stress test is better read as **evidence for** the concurrent series than
against it. Four applications each demanded a *different* construct — interval
addressing, predicate-selected update, `sum`, `argMax`, interval membership,
append-only — which is precisely the failure mode a registry-shaped surface predicts
and a general algebra is meant to avoid. K1's "two ladders" finding is the same
observation reached from measurement: appending E, F, G to a rung list produces the
unordered wish list LLP 0329.013 diagnoses.

What this document adds that the algebra papers do not have is the **falsifying
evidence** — four archetypes, three model families, and a specific refuted security
rule (K2). What it cannot do is adjudicate whether the bound-carrying algebra
actually covers these six constructs; that is the obvious next test and is listed as
an open question.

## What survives

**Finding 1 is confirmed and unshaken.** The point-read restriction is the first
blocker in 9 of booking's 11 mutations, and all four apps hit it. No document
questions it.

**The direction is confirmed.** A bounded, indexed, visibility-composed read into a
plan binding is the right primitive. Booking independently verified that its machinery
is already in the executor with a *richer* predicate vocabulary than the proposal
asked for — matching the correction recorded in LLP 0329.006 F4.

**Rungs A–D are real for row-shaped work.** The ledger's verdict is representative:
"A is real … B is real … C is real and Finding-3-clean for equal-split member count …
D is real."

## Findings

### K1 — The ladder measures one axis, and there are two ladders

The most valuable structural correction, stated most sharply by the workspace report:

> **E and F are not further rungs on the same ladder.** The ladder's rungs are all the
> same primitive at different `take` values, which is why LLP 0329.008's result was so
> satisfying. E and F are not. E is a write construct; F is an expression construct;
> both are orthogonal to read width. If they get appended to the same list, the list
> stops being "one primitive at four settings" and becomes an unordered wish list, and
> the elegance of the original finding — which is real — gets used to justify a shape
> it does not have.

Booking reaches the same place from measurement rather than taxonomy: "They are the
same primitive at different `take` values *along one axis*. My hardest read is
`take(1)` and unreachable. **Cardinality is not the variable that matters.**"

The honest presentation is **two ladders plus orthogonal axes**:

- **Read width** (A–D) — one primitive at four `take` values. Coterie-derived, correct.
- **Write shape** — single row → bounded set → predicate set. `deleteWhere`-derived,
  and **half-built**: the IR has the destructive form and not the constructive one.
- **Orthogonal axes** that are not rungs at all: expression vocabulary (aggregates,
  containment), schema constraints, scheduled/system execution authority, and interval
  membership.

### K2 — LLP 0329.011's count rule is refuted, and fails toward silent wrongness

Three of the four found this independently, and it is the most consequential result.

The rule admitted a count when the domain is (a) filtered by the caller's read
visibility and (b) keyed to a resource. Booking's counterexample:

> "How many people are ahead of me on the waitlist" … (b) passes: the domain is
> `waitlistEntries where resourceId = R ∧ state = 'waiting' ∧ position < mine`. (a)
> "passes," and destroys the answer. To a plain member the visible domain is their own
> rows. The count returns 0 or 1. **The count is admitted and returns a wrong number.**

The auction found the identical shape: "owner visibility gives the buyer only zero or
one row, so a globally full auction appears not full." The workspace independently
classified it as "a correctness bug, not just an incomplete feature."

This directly answers LLP 0329.011's own open question 3 — *"worth confirming that
visibility-filtered counts never turn a refusal into a silent pass"* — and the answer
is worse than the question anticipated: **they do not merely pass silently, they
return a confidently wrong integer.** That is the silent-degradation class this whole
family treats as disqualifying, arriving *through* the rule written to prevent it.

**The correction.** Clause (a) is wrong as a general rule. Visibility filtering is
right for a count that is *about* the caller and wrong for a count that is *about a
resource*. The workspace's framing is the fix: rule 3 is a **policy-count** rule, not
a count rule. A structural count — waitlist depth, auction fullness, split coverage —
is an integrity question about the resource, must be computed over the *unfiltered*
domain, and must therefore be admitted by a different route: either an elevated
resource-scoped count with a declared permission, or a public denormalized counter
maintained in the same transaction (the auction's answer, and the honest product
choice when the number is not itself secret).

### K3 — The largest single miss is a set-oriented *write*

Named independently by three reports, and the asymmetry is stark: the IR ships
`DeleteWhere` — predicate-selected destruction — and has **no constructive twin**.

- Workspace: `patchWhere` closes **6 of its 12** operations and is its top-ranked miss.
- Auction: `patchEach`/`deleteEach` alongside `insertEach`; settlement patches each
  hold and wallet.
- Ledger: `patchEach` with a **per-row expression** — "D as specified (insertEach of a
  fixed shape) is not enough."

The ledger adds a dependency worth flagging: fan-out patch is what maintains
materialized aggregates, and without it "settlement and every UI prediction becomes a
sum over unbounded history."

### K4 — Aggregates beyond `count`

- **Sum** (ledger): "This is the rung the archetype was built to demand." Without it,
  the split-coverage invariant is unenforceable in a plan, and writes escape *because
  the invariant is a sum*.
- **Ranking / `argMax` / `topK`** (auction): "`highest bid wins` is not a count."
- **Stable ordering and map-with-index** (ledger, auction): money division needs a
  deterministic remainder, and "non-deterministic remainder breaks optimistic
  prediction even after C exists."

### K5 — Interval membership recurs in every single app

The coterie trap generalizes and is the most repeated finding in the set: booking's
cancelled-but-historical reservations and expiring holds, the workspace's inherited
grants and share-link expiry, the auction's sealed-until-close bids, the ledger's
"former member × new expense is the coterie bug."

The ledger states the shape precisely: `roles: ['member', 'former']` **is not an
interval**. A role enum cannot express membership-at-a-time.

### K6 — Schema constraints must bind JS, or they are a convention

The ledger's sharpest line: **"`E` without `G` means the one mutation you did not
rewrite as a plan can still patch a posting."** Append-only, composite uniqueness, and
similar integrity constraints are permissions on *operations*. If they bind only
declarative plans, an author escapes to a handler and the constraint evaporates —
which is exactly the dynamic this family has been measuring all along, now applied to
integrity rather than capability.

The workspace generalizes it: the unique index is the existing mechanism, and the
right move is to generalize *that* — host-evaluated, unfiltered, sealed refusal —
rather than to widen the bounded read.

### K7 — Scheduled and system authority is an orthogonal axis

The auction's top-ranked miss is not a read at all: **read elevation cannot settle.**
A scheduled close must atomically patch bounded owner rows under system authority,
with transactional enqueue, generation fencing, system-only invocation, and repair
semantics. Its verdict is explicit that this "is not honestly rung G" but a different
axis, and that the current alternative is "a hand-audited public JS handler using
`ctx.db.elevated`."

### K8 — One positive surprise: the concurrency story is better than believed

Booking predicted a hole in interval concurrency, checked, and found the opposite:

> range-granular capture is shipped and predicate-complete on the handler path, and
> plan steps run inside the commit transaction, so a plan-side overlap check would be
> serializable by construction. **This is a stronger position than the corpus
> currently believes it is in.**

Recorded because the corpus's honesty discipline runs mostly toward finding
over-claims, and this is an under-claim.

## Where the product requirement should change instead

The brief asked each report to say when the right fix is the product, not the backend.
The clearest guidance:

- **Auction (strongest):** do not add clock-driven visibility to the grammar. "Keep
  authorization time-independent and turn the deadline into a durable state transition
  that invalidates live queries." This converts the head-on collision with
  `SNAPBACK_AUTH_CLOCK_DEPENDENT_VISIBILITY` into an ordinary write.
- **Workspace:** share-link expiry becomes "stops working within a few minutes of
  5:00pm, and revocation is instant" — an availability claim rather than a
  clock-exact authorization claim.
- **Ledger:** "maybe the ledger should not allow retroactive edits" is a coherent
  product. Its report includes a table of eight such trades and when each is a cheat;
  the boundary it draws is that a product change is illegitimate when it is adopted
  *to protect the backend model* rather than because the simpler product is genuinely
  wanted — and specifically when you still claim offline retention, because then "the
  mutations that maintain books cannot be the opaque 74%."

## Consequences

1. **LLP 0329.008's headline needs restating.** "One new step plus two extensions
   closes 100%" is true of `coterie` and false of every other archetype tested. The
   defensible claim is that it closes 100% of *row-shaped* work in a messaging app.
2. **LLP 0329.011's count rule must be narrowed to policy counts** before it is
   relied on. As written it endorses a class of silently wrong answers.
3. **The next construct to spec is probably `patchWhere`, not a wider read.** It is
   named by three of four reports, it has an existing destructive twin to copy, and
   the workspace measured it at half its operations.
4. **The escape-rate metric will not move for whole archetypes** — ACL apps, ledgers —
   no matter how far the read ladder goes, because their blockers are writes,
   aggregates, and constraints.

## Confidence

High that the four reports say what is quoted; they are committed verbatim as
sub-documents.

Medium-high on K2, which is the strongest result: three independent derivations of the
same counterexample class, and the failure mode is checkable by inspection rather than
by measurement.

Medium on K1's two-ladder framing. It is a taxonomy proposal from one report that the
others corroborate implicitly rather than adopt; it is persuasive but not measured.

**Low on the per-app percentages (27%, 0 of 12, and so on).** These are
self-classifications by the model that designed the application, against a ladder
supplied in the prompt. They are not independently audited, and the apps are
plausible-looking designs rather than working code — none was run through codegen, and
LLP 0329.009 showed codegen accepts constructs that are semantically wrong. Treat the
*classes* of missing construct as the finding and the numbers as illustration.

A further caution: each report was told the model it was testing and asked to find
where it breaks. That framing invites confirmation of breakage. The convergence across
three model families on specific, checkable counterexamples (K2 especially) is what
makes me believe the findings rather than the framing.

## Open questions

1. Does `patchWhere` have the same "mechanism already exists" property that
   LLP 0329.010 H5 found for the bounded read? `DeleteWhere` already builds a
   `QueryPlan` and materializes rows; a patch would reuse that and write instead of
   delete.
2. What is the right admission route for a structural (non-policy) count, given K2?
   Elevated resource-scoped count, or maintained public counter, or both?
3. Is interval membership (K5) a visibility-grammar extension or a schema feature
   (`.interval({ from, to })`)? Four apps needed it; none of them could express it.
4. Do these findings replicate on working code? Every number here comes from static
   self-classification of designs that were never generated or run.
5. **Does the bound-carrying algebra of LLP 0329.012/0329.014 cover the six
   constructs K3–K6 name** — predicate-selected update, `sum`, `argMax`/stable
   ordering, interval membership, append-only, and system/scheduled write authority?
   The four applications in this family are the natural test corpus for that, and
   testing it is cheaper than designing a seventh admitted shape.
