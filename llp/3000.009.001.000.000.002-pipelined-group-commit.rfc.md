## 1. Summary

On the one-host S1 runner Snapback's group committer runs one batch at a
time. A batch averages N≈16 of its 32-member cap, makes five (G5) or six
(G6) serial PG flights and pays a ~2.6 ms durable WAL sync before the next
batch may begin. That caps W4 near 1.1k writes/s; the final comparison
qualifies 800/s on both links against the Supabase advocate's 3,200 (A) and
1,600 (B). Strictly beating the controls needs 4,525/s on A and 2,262/s on B.

This RFC designs **G7, a pipelined group commit for the Postgres store**.
While batch k's final message (apply, tail, COMMIT) is in flight and its WAL
flush is running, batch k+1 is collected, fetches its facts and executes on
a second writer connection, reading through k's frozen, uncommitted overlay.
k+1's commit is conditional on k through a **commit ticket**, a store-keyed
transaction advisory lock that k takes in its first flight and k+1 waits on
in its final message, followed by an exact **compare-and-set of `_head`**
against the values and **per-attempt nonce** k leaves. Recovery **drains**
every dispatched transaction before it re-seeds or reruns. Only buffered
G3b waves pipeline; every other producer is a **barrier**. Results,
receipts, continuations and publication are released in revision order.

Three findings shape what this design can promise:

1. **Synchronously committed ordered batches cannot share a flush.** A
   synchronous commit releases locks only after its flush, so whatever makes
   k+1 wait for k wakes it after k's flush: one sync per batch (§2.3). Until
   G7.4 the chain is apply plus COMMIT, 5.0–5.6 ms on A, and binds there
   (§5.2).
2. **Early lock release lifts that limit, at an admissibility cost.** Arm E
   commits k with `synchronous_commit=off` and a same-message, count-checked
   marker transaction that sets `synchronous_commit=on` itself: locks
   release before the flush, and no reply or reader output is released
   until a flush certified in the current recovery epoch covers its head
   identity; after any break a synchronous fenced commit rebuilds the
   durable point (§3.8–§3.9). Its depth-1 form, E1, needs no row fold or
   ticket chain but still speculates on engine state. The S1 method fixes
   `synchronous_commit`; ruling 8 (§10) admits E with conditions and builds
   it after G7.2, because G7.0 measured S's B slack (≈0.7 ms) below the
   product's HTTP intake.
3. **With 32 closed-loop clients, latency binds.** The rate cannot exceed
   32/L: L ≤ 7.07 ms on A and ≤ 14.15 ms on B (§2.2). A pipeline removes
   the wait for the previous batch, not a request's own flights or sync.

Planning arithmetic, not measurement (§5): G7.2 roughly doubles W4 to
about 2.0k (A) and 1.9k (B). With the two-flight final (G7.3) and two
levers named elsewhere (non-authorizing `/m` ingress, committer CPU near
100 µs/member), B reaches about 2.5k, making **the B rungs (W4/B, W5 mix/B
at 2,262) plausible without G7.4 or E**, since (X+C)/2, not the chain,
binds there; A's modelled floor, about 7.8 ms, makes **W4/A a forecast
loss under every ordered arm** (§5.4). G7.0 measures arms S, E and C first.

**Decision (accepted):** build in §8.1's order: the cold-reader convoy fix
(lane R1, elsewhere), since no gain qualifies while first native
repetitions fail counter equality; G7.0 (arms S/E/C, K/H split); G7.1,
still synchronous (5/6 flights to 3); G7.2; G7.3 with the two levers; then
the B rungs. G7.4 or arm E, like G7.5, is built only if the measured chain
binds on B or d=3 is wanted. Rulings 1–6 are decided, 7 waits on G7.0, 8
is deferred (§10).

## 2. The measured problem

### 2.1 One batch at a time

The diagnosis traced S1 writes at offered 1,131/s; its write-attribution
table gives mean wall time per batch under G6. "Residual" is the cycle
minus timed phases: the two opener flights and untimed committer CPU.

| Phase | W4-A ms | W4-B ms | Flights |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Residual: opener + untimed committer work | 4.47 | 5.37 | 2 |
| Facts: composed authority, receipts, level 0 (`batch.rs:64–84`) | 1.79 | 2.23 | 1 |
| Parallel interpretation (G3b-par) | 0.62 | 0.73 | 0 |
| Apply + vector tail (`group_tail.rs:6–45`) | 2.66 | 2.78 | 1 |
| G6 publication witness (`read_grant.rs:64`, `store.rs:744`) | 0.75 | 1.24 | 1 |
| COMMIT, including ≈2.6 ms WAL sync | 3.45 | 3.85 | 1 |
| **Cycle** (mean N 15.67 / 15.98) | **13.73** | **16.19** | **6** |

A PG flight costs R ≈ 0.55 ms on A and 1.10 ms on B (the diagnosis prices
two saved flights at 1.1/2.2 ms). Charging the opener flights at R+0.1 ms
each leaves **K ≈ 3.1 ms per batch of unattributed committer CPU**, about
195 µs/member (G3b-par loopback measured 99.5 µs/member at N≈32 without
HTTP); K is inferred from non-additive means, and G7.0 measures it. Two
groups of about 16 clients alternate, each waiting for the other's cycle
and then its own (≈987/s on B): the serial schedule sets the rate.

### 2.2 What 7.07 ms and 14.15 ms mean

S1 drives 32 write clients, each with at most one request outstanding, so
**the sustained rate is at most 32/L**, L the mean send-to-response time:
4,525/s needs L ≤ 7.07 ms (A), 2,262/s L ≤ 14.15 ms (B), or 6.4 and 12.7
ms at about 90% client occupancy. Under any schedule `L = H + I_ing + Q +
(batch execution) + (commit) + (release)`: H is HTTP and generator time,
I_ing the warm authority statement `ingress_of`
(`crates/snapback4/src/serve_identity.rs:8`) per mutation outside the
batch, Q the wait to be collected. A pipeline cannot relax 32/L.

### 2.3 What a pipeline removes, and what it cannot

Today Q is about one cycle. A pipeline cuts Q to about half a batch
interval and overlaps one batch's flights and CPU with another's flush.
What it cannot do, **while every batch commits synchronously**, is merge
flushes: a synchronous commit flushes WAL to its commit record before it
releases its locks, so whatever makes k+1 wait for k (a row, advisory lock
or head tuple) wakes it after k's flush; if k+1 does not wait, it can
become durable while k aborts, a gap below head. Flushes are shared only by
transactions that do not wait on each other (option C) or by ordered
batches whose locks release before their flush (arm E, §3.8). So **the
synchronous chain carries one serialized WAL sync per batch**, plus apply
until G7.4 (§5.2). Every request still pays its own facts flight, final
flight and sync, plus queueing, modelled as Q ≈ I/2 for uniform phases: a
model, not a bound, since closed-loop clients return in bursts.

## 3. Design

### 3.1 Shape, eligibility and barriers

- **Depth.** At most d batches are in flight, from first flight until
  release. The baseline is **d = 2**; **d = 3** is conditional on §5.3.
- **Pipelined batches.** Only buffered G3b waves (`program_batch.rs:268–273`)
  whose members the bounded closure selects: existing rows, unchanged index
  keys, no inserts, deletes, allocations, maintained or capped writes, and
  no time- or presence-dependent programs (controls §6, G3b note). They
  write only those rows' `r` images, the tail tables (§3.5), `_ops` and
  `_head`; a late unknown family that would take G3b's serial recovery
  aborts the attempt before dispatch and reruns as a barrier.
- **Barriers.** Every other producer: Engine, Publication, Direct,
  administrative and long members, non-buffered program groups, activation
  and adoption. A barrier waits until every in-flight batch is released,
  then runs at depth 1 through today's opener (`store.rs:314–322`, `:359`,
  with `_head FOR UPDATE`) and tail, which also writes a fresh head nonce
  (§3.4). Session mints and revocations are Direct members, so **authority
  changes are always barriers**.
- **N = 1 and collection.** An idle lone member keeps today's solo route
  (`program_batch.rs:55–57`), else pipelines as a one-member wave.
  Collection is unchanged (`command_batch.rs:206–237`); a batch starts once
  its predecessor's final message is out and a slot frees.

### 3.2 The flights of a pipelined batch

**P1, one message** (replacing today's BEGIN+fence, head/generation/
authority and facts flights):

1. `BEGIN ISOLATION LEVEL READ COMMITTED READ WRITE` and the settings guard
   (`flight.rs:35–61`).
2. The owner fence, `SELECT store_id, token FROM _owner … FOR KEY SHARE`,
   as the first command (`store.rs:183`).
3. **The ticket**: `pg_advisory_xact_lock(t(store, k mod 64))` (§3.4).
4. Generation and `_head` (seq, revision, attempt), read **without** a lock
   as the authority SQL already does (`batch.rs:28`), and
   `pg_current_xact_id()` for transaction-status witnesses (§8.2).
5. The composed authority, receipt/placement and level-0 statements
   (`batch.rs:18–41`, `:64–84`). Authority is read once.

The fence check stays client-side; every later command is a lock or a read,
so a refused fence leaves nothing behind (round-trip §3.5). Dependent levels
remain composed level flights. **CPU** then binds, folds the chain (§3.3),
interprets in parallel, accepts in revision order and builds the tail.

**Final message.** In G7.2, three-flight, one message, then COMMIT:

1. `SET LOCAL statement_timeout` and `lock_timeout` to the earliest
   member's remaining deadline (§4.4).
2. Wait on the predecessor's ticket.
3. The nonce CAS (§3.4) and a generation and identity check that also
   captures publication, each its own command after the wait, so each takes
   a fresh READ COMMITTED snapshot (round-trip §3.5).
4. Bulk apply plus the vector tail, with the existing counts
   (`group_tail.rs:222–227`).

The client verifies the counts, then sends `SET LOCAL statement_timeout=0;
SET LOCAL lock_timeout=0; COMMIT` alone (`store.rs:746–756`). In G7.3,
two-flight, the same message continues with raising count assertions and
that reset plus COMMIT (controls §10.7's qualified variant). A 55P03 at the
ticket wait, a failed CAS or a raised assertion fails the transaction;
PostgreSQL skips the rest of the message, COMMIT included, and the client's
ROLLBACK is acknowledged (ruling 4, §8.2 item 5). That is **3 flights per
batch (G7.2) or 2 (G7.3), independent of N**, against 5 (G5) and 6 (G6).

### 3.3 Reading through uncommitted predecessors

When batch j has accepted its last member, the committer freezes a
**pending overlay P_j**: the final image of every row j writes (the
`accepted` map, `wave.rs:293–301`); every receipt and refusal receipt j
records, keyed by `(principal, id)`, with its complete binding and outcome;
the `_head` (seq, revision, attempt) j will leave; and §3.7's provisional
state after j's last member.

The **chain** of batch k is the set of batches not yet released when k's P1
is dispatched, at most d−1. Before k interprets, the committer **folds** it,
in revision order, into k's batch base and receipt facts: chain images and
receipts replace fetched ones. The existing lookup (`overlay.rs:71–100`)
and conflict detection (`wave.rs:262–292`) run unchanged.

**Why the fold is exact.** Between barriers, under the fence, only
pipelined batches write, so any snapshot P1 takes sees the committed state
after some prefix of k's chain. A key a chain member writes gets its latest
pending value from the fold; every committed state in that range agrees on
other keys. The folded base is therefore the state after k−1, however the
commits interleave with P1, and the controls RFC's induction (§3.3, "Why
serial-equivalent") extends verbatim: k's members are members N+1, N+2, …
of a merged batch k−1 ∪ k, with the rules, refusals, meters, images,
receipts, effective times and sequence positions of serial reference `run`.
The first slice relies on G3b's selector: no absence, range or index entry
can change, so the fold is a pure replacement and `covers_span`
(`wave.rs:274`) is unaffected; a later family joins only once its fold rule
qualifies under the same differential.

**Authority stays fresh.** Program members cannot write session, identity,
generation or `_meta` state, so k's P1 authority statement is the final
authority read inside k's fenced transaction (round-trip §3.1, §7), with
deadlines checked at admission (committer §3.6) and again just before the
final message. Pending images stay within each batch's 32 MiB reservation.

### 3.4 The commit ticket and the nonce CAS

**Why `_head FOR UPDATE` must leave the opener.** Every writer takes it
first today (`store.rs:315`, `:359`), so k+1's P1 would wait for k's
COMMIT. **A head CAS alone is not enough**: two connections are not ordered
on the server, so k+1's final message can reach `_head` before k's tail
updates it (`group_tail.rs:7`), or lock a row k is about to update.

**The ticket.** Each batch takes a transaction advisory lock T_k in P1. It
writes no WAL and is released only when the transaction ends, by commit,
abort or termination. k+1's final message is never dispatched before k's P1
returns, because k+1's interpretation needs P_k, so k holds T_k before k+1
waits on it, and k+1 proceeds only after k has ended. **Keys derive from
store identity**: one 64-bit key, 58 bits from `_meta.store_id` above 6
bits of `k mod 64`. Slots of one store never collide; stores sharing a
database collide only if their ids share those bits, adding a wait or a
cross-store cycle (§3.5), never a wrong commit. Batch k+64 cannot start
until k has ended (d ≤ 3).

**The CAS.** `_head` gains an **`attempt`** pair: the owner token and a
per-ownership counter that only rises in memory (§3.6). Tokens rise on every
acquisition (`ownership.rs:313–335`), so no value repeats. Every `_head`
writer, barriers and reruns included, sets a fresh attempt. k+1 runs `UPDATE
_head SET seq=$s1, revision=$r1, attempt=$a1 WHERE singleton AND seq=$s0 AND
revision=$r0 AND attempt=$a0 RETURNING …` with k's leaving values. As a
fresh statement after the wait, it matches **if and only if k's own attempt
committed**; a rerun or replacement with equal numeric end values cannot
satisfy it (the ABA case of §4.2). Zero rows aborts k+1, by raising in G7.3
or by the client's count check in G7.2, reporting the observed head (§4.3).
The CAS replaces the seq-only compare (`group_tail.rs:7`) and the relative
`revision = revision + n` (`revision.rs:80–101`).

### 3.5 Other serializing rows, and deadlock

| Row or table | Today | Under G7 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `_head` | `FOR UPDATE` at open; seq CAS and revision bump in the tail | Unlocked read in P1; one nonce CAS after the ticket wait; contended only after an early end (below) |
| `_change_evidence` prune | `DELETE … WHERE seq <= last−1000` (`group_tail.rs:22`); ranges overlap | `seq > s0−1000 AND seq <= s1−1000`: disjoint across in-flight batches |
| `_commits`, `_log`, `_outbox`, `_effect_presence` | Inserts | Unchanged; seq ranges are disjoint per batch |
| `_ops` | Insert of the bound receipt | Duplicates across batches resolve through the fold; a key collision is a 23505 known abort |
| `r` images | Bulk update of final images | A row both k and k+1 write is updated by k+1 only after the ticket wait |
| `_owner` | Key share; renewal `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` | Unchanged and compatible (store-port §5.1) |
| `_meta`, `_sequences`, maintained owner rows, cap rows | Written by some families | Not written by first-slice members; their writers stay barriers |

**Within one store the wait graph is acyclic.** Before its ticket wait a
pipelined batch holds only the fence's key share and its own ticket, and
**every wait points at a lower batch**, normally the predecessor's ticket.
If that predecessor ends early while an older one runs (at d=3, k+1's
55P03 while k runs), k+2's CAS or row update waits on k's lock, then
re-evaluates against k's head and fails: an innocent abort, not a cycle. No batch waits on
a higher one, and recovery probes take tickets highest first (§4.2). G7.4's
**pre-wait disjoint apply** writes, before the wait, only rows provably
absent from every chain member's write set, shrinking the chain to roughly
the sync without touching any row an in-flight batch writes.

**Across stores it is not.** A ticket-key collision between stores sharing
a database, or a foreign advisory-lock user, can close a cycle; PostgreSQL's
detector then aborts one transaction with 40P01, which the commit path
already classifies as a known abort (`store.rs:719–724`). **Nothing here
claims 40P01 cannot occur** (§8.2 item 7).

### 3.6 Sequencer, in-order release and the publication gate

**The sequencer.** The committer assigns each batch its seq range, revision
range and attempt in memory, from its predecessor's end. The CAS'd head is
the durable truth; seq and revision in memory are a cache, like the
`_sequences` counters (committer §4.5), re-seeded from `_head` at every
barrier and, after an abort or unknown outcome, **only after drainage**
(§4.2). **The attempt counter is never re-seeded downward**: it rises in
memory for the ownership, and every attempt, dispatched or not, takes a
fresh value; re-seeding it from k−1's head would give k's rerun k's nonce.

**In-order release.** Confirmations can arrive out of order on two sockets;
continuations, replies, `committed` and `/changes` wakeups, G5 installs,
`BatchStats` and published state are released **strictly in revision
order**, k+1's waiting until k is **resolved** (every member classified,
unknown included; §4.3). In the database visibility order is revision
order, since k becomes visible before it releases T_k, so readers,
`partition::stream` and effect claimers only ever see contiguous prefixes.

**The G6 gate.** Today closure is the state write lock held across one
COMMIT (`read_grant.rs:189–240`), which `read()` sees as a failed
`try_read` (`read_grant.rs:147–181`). Two slot threads cannot share that
guard, so closure becomes an **`inflight` count that `read()` checks**:

- **Close before dispatch.** Before any final message, the committer takes
  the write lock, which waits for in-progress hits to drop their read
  guards (today's synchronized admission), increments `inflight`, releases.
- **Readers see it, off the guard.** `read()` checks `inflight` under its
  read guard; if it is non-zero, `read()` **drops the guard**, waits up to
  ruling 5's short bound on a condition the committer signals on reopening,
  then re-checks or returns `None` and the read takes G5. Waiting under the
  guard would block the write lock the decrement needs.
- **Install in order, then reopen.** On each resolution the committer takes
  the write lock, installs in revision order every confirmed publication
  whose lower batches are resolved (under E, only by §3.8's release
  rule), and decrements for each resolved batch. It reopens only
  at `inflight = 0` with nothing uncertain.
- **Sticky uncertainty.** An unknown outcome sets `uncertain`, clears epoch
  and `published` and pauses writer admission until fenced reacquisition
  with a fresh epoch (`read_grant.rs:222–227`). A known abort decrements
  without installing; a panic sets `uncertain`, as today.

Publication capture moves into the final message's identity check,
replacing the `PUBLICATION` query (`store.rs:744`). S1's write cells have
no G6 hits. **Adaptive N** (`command_batch.rs:172–203`) keeps its p99
controller, ticket waits included.

### 3.7 Provisional engine state

Everything a batch's members or finish mutate is provisional until release.
On a cascade or unknown outcome it is discarded back to the last released
batch, and the next attempt starts there. E1 chains it through P_k too,
since k+1 interprets before k is certified (§3.8); §8.2 item 4 witnesses it.

### 3.8 Arm E: early lock release (conditional; needs ruling 8)

§2.3's finding holds only for synchronous commits. Under E, k's locks and
ticket release when its commit record is inserted, before the flush, and
durability is certified separately. k's final message ends `SET LOCAL
synchronous_commit=off; SET LOCAL statement_timeout=0; SET LOCAL
lock_timeout=0; COMMIT; BEGIN; SET LOCAL synchronous_commit=on; UPDATE
_durable SET n = n + 1 WHERE token = $t AND slot = $s; COMMIT`. The last
transaction, a **durable marker** on one row per slot and owner token, sets
`synchronous_commit=on` itself: the session default is never relied on, and
the settings guard (`flight.rs:35–61`) does not check it. It adds no
flight; an error before k's COMMIT skips it.

**The certificate.** A marker certifies k only as a committed,
WAL-generating write: **`UPDATE 1`**, then its COMMIT tag, ending at
ReadyForQuery `I` with no error and no "canceling wait" warning. A zero-row
update takes no xid, and PostgreSQL does not flush a commit without one, so
`UPDATE 0`, like a marker error or a missing reply, certifies nothing
(§4.1). The marker's commit record follows k's, so its flush, and any
synchronous standby's, covers k. **Barriers, the solo route, recovery and
takeover commit synchronously**, each with `SET LOCAL synchronous_commit=on`
in its own transaction, and their confirmed commits are certificates.

**The marker is unfenced**, an exception to store-port §5's fence-first
rule. `_durable`'s contents are never read, only the marker's tags; an old
owner's late marker after takeover still tells the truth (WAL through k's
commit record is flushed) and moves no D the new owner uses (§3.9); and
each acquisition provisions its own token's rows by insert only, so it
never waits behind an old marker (stale rows are pruned `SKIP LOCKED`).

**The durable point D** is (ε, a_ε, D.r, D.s): §3.9's recovery epoch, the
attempt its recovery commit wrote, the durable revision and the **durable
public head** `_head.seq` at D.r, so a seq is never compared with a
revision. Within an epoch, a certificate for batch j raises D to j's end:
j's CAS or locked head read observed its predecessor's attempt, so every
lower commit record precedes j's and j's flush covers them.

**Identity and the one release rule.** Every held output carries the head
identity (r, a), revision and attempt, it depends on, **read from the
database**: a batch's own end, or for a reader the `_head` (seq, revision,
attempt) read inside its own snapshot (same statement, or first in a
REPEATABLE READ transaction), never an epoch sampled around it. Attempts
only rise, across recoveries and acquisitions too (§3.4, §3.6), and any
break bumps the epoch, so within one epoch the history is linear and
(r, a) lies on it if a ≥ a_ε. **An output is released only when,
atomically under the committer's release lock, a ≥ a_ε and D covers r**
(public seqs against D.s). A bump takes that lock: what D covered is
released, every other held output and pending G5/G6 fill is discarded. A
batch's outputs (replies, continuations, receipts, G5 installs, G6
publication, `/changes` wakeups) release in revision order once confirmed
on their own connection (§4.1); a successor's certificate raises D but
confirms no caller (ruling 3).

**Readers, by construction.** Under E the PG reader transport is the only
path from a reader statement to rows, and returns them only as head-bound
values that open through the release rule: reader-pool queries, G5 fills,
sync expansion through `partition::stream`, keyed receipt replay from
`_ops`, caches and claimers alike. A snapshot reader holds until D covers
**its own head**, not the highest revision dispatched. Range readers
(claimers, `/changes` pages, sync cursors) also bound at `seq ≤ D.s`, and
any mutable dependency they read is head-bound too; G5 hits serve only
entries installed at certified identities. A visible head is
committed, so a certificate, barrier or recovery commit covers it, or the
epoch ends and the hold is discarded; past a measured hold bound a reader
fails retryably. The hold costs up to one flush of read latency plus a head
read per statement, which S1's mixed cells must price; it replaces controls
§4.1's argument that a G5 hit linearizes because `synchronous_commit=on`.

**Depth-1 form, E1.** k+1's P1 goes out on the second slot as soon as k's
final message does, with G7.1's fused opener but **today's `_head FOR
UPDATE`** after the fence. That wait wakes at k's lock release, before k's
flush and reply, so k+1 reads committed rows (no fold, ticket wait or
successor witness) but is **not speculation-free**: its locked head read
decides. k's attempt means k committed, and k+1 takes its §3.7 state from
P_k; k−1's means k ended uncommitted, and k+1 rolls back to k−1's state and
reruns after k's survivors in original order (§4.2). An unknown k rolls an
undispatched k+1 back and bumps the epoch. The nonce CAS stays as the head
update, §3.6's G6 count covers both slots, and the two-flight final carries
G7.3's raising assertions from the start (ruling 4).

**With the chain.** At d ≥ 2 E shortens the chain to apply plus an
asynchronous commit, about 2.4 / 1.8 ms: G7.4's purpose without its
overlap set, so **if either is built, E replaces G7.4**.

**Admissibility.** S1 method §4 fixes `synchronous_commit` on and labels a
`synchronous_commit=off` cell a relaxed row. E keeps `postgresql.conf` and
acknowledged durability, but sets the knob per transaction and exposes
non-durable rows to raw clients. Whether E is a headline cell, and whether
every control's advocate may use it, is ruling 8: admitted with the
conditions in §10, under S1 §4 as amended.

### 3.9 Arm E: recovery identity

A numeric D is not an identity: if PostgreSQL crashes and loses an
uncertified revision r a reader holds, the next batch reuses r, and
`D.r ≥ r` would release lost output or certify a lost G5 fill. Keyed by
§3.8's (r, a), the lost r's attempt is below the next epoch's a_ε, so the
reuse never releases it. ε is a process-local epoch that never decreases.

**Epoch bump.** ε rises on any involuntary loss of a store connection or
changed `pg_postmaster_start_time`, a Snapback restart (no D), any unknown
or committed-unconfirmed outcome (§4.1) and every ownership acquisition.
The committer pauses admission, under G6 sets `uncertain` (§3.6), and
applies the release rule a last time (§3.8). Readers retry in the new
epoch. A dispatched batch above D settles by its own terminal result
during the drain: an acknowledged abort reruns after recovery; anything
else, a late certificate included, is `E_COMMIT_OUTCOME_UNKNOWN` with no
automatic rerun. An undispatched batch reruns.

**Drain is not durability.** §4.2's evidence (a terminal result, a ticket
probe, E1's `_head FOR UPDATE` wake, takeover's lock) proves a transaction
ended, and under E it ends at its commit-record insert, before its flush.
Drain gates re-seeding only; **D is never seeded from a head read**.

**The recovery commit.** After the drain, the fenced recovery barrier takes
`_head FOR UPDATE`, reads head H, writes a fresh attempt a_ε (one row,
count checked) and **commits with `SET LOCAL synchronous_commit=on`**; its
commit record follows every commit visible to it, so its flush covers H.
Only on its confirmed COMMIT does D become (ε, a_ε, H.revision, H.seq) and
the epoch release output or admit writers; a failed or unknown recovery
commit bumps again. A lost last marker with no later writer is an unknown,
hence a bump and a recovery commit, so every path ends.

**Ownership loss.** Takeover's `FOR UPDATE` on `_owner` is granted only
once every old key share is gone (store-port §5.2), and a committed old
batch k drops its share at its commit-record insert, so takeover's
synchronous commit flushes over k even when it kills k's marker. The new
owner's epoch releases nothing before its recovery commit. The old owner
latches loss, applies the release rule a last time and, unable to run a
fenced recovery, **refuses**: no further batch output, and every read
whose head exceeds its last D fails retryably. k's callers get unknown and
resolve keyed retries through `_ops` on the new owner once its D covers
them.

**Crash and loss.** Recovery replays a WAL prefix, so the head stays
contiguous. No reply precedes a certificate in its epoch, and after any
break nothing is released until a synchronous recovery commit covers it, so
**no acknowledged write can be lost and no Snapback output escapes a lost
history**. A lost unacknowledged suffix leaves its callers
`E_COMMIT_OUTCOME_UNKNOWN`, as today. **A raw PostgreSQL client outside
Snapback can see rows a crash later loses**; Snapback's own readers cannot.

### 3.10 Writer slots, driver and the connection budget

- **Slots.** d writer connections, each tagged `sb:<store>:<holder>:<token>`
  as today, so takeover terminates all of them (store-port §5.2). Under the
  matched cap of 32: 1 owner/lease + d writers + (31−d) readers, **29
  readers at d=2 or E1, 28 at d=3**, against store-port §8's 30 (ruling 1).
- **Driver.** Each slot keeps the synchronous `postgres` client on its own
  thread, off the PIPE runtime (ruling 6). The committer thread never blocks
  on a socket and alone owns `&mut Engine`; today's `Tx<'a>` borrows the
  store mutably (`store.rs:401–429`), so slot transactions become owned and
  `Send`.

## 4. Outcomes and faults

### 4.1 Outcome table

Controls §3.1, the store-port §5.2 rows and §10.7's two-flight table apply
to each batch on its own connection. G7 adds cross-batch rows. In E1, k+1's
locked head read shows whether k committed; if not, k+1 rolls back and
reruns after k's survivors in original order, and an unknown k follows the
first column and bumps the epoch (§3.8–§3.9).

| k's outcome | Successor k+1 not yet dispatched | Successor k+1 dispatched (waiting on T_k, or later) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmed | k+1 proceeds | k+1 is classified on its own connection |
| Known abort (explicit error, acknowledged ROLLBACK) | Roll k+1 back: a known abort with no failed member | T_k is released; k+1's CAS finds k−1's attempt and raises; COMMIT is skipped and ROLLBACK acknowledged: a known abort. Drain before any rerun (§4.2) |
| Unknown (lost reply, timeout, terminated during COMMIT, unacknowledged rollback) | Roll k+1 back (known); drain; re-seed (§4.3) | k+1 resolves on its own connection; its evidence reconciles internal state, not k's callers (ruling 3) |
| Ownership lost | Do not dispatch; roll back; `E_STORE_OWNERSHIP` | Actual result; the fence orders it before takeover; termination after dispatch is unknown |

**Under E**, k's final message carries two transactions, classified by
**transaction status**: ReadyForQuery state and command tags, or
`pg_xact_status` of k's P1 xid, asked on the same connection or, once the
connection is lost, on another connection after the drain shows k's backend
ended, where "in progress" counts as unknown; **never by error code
alone**, and **any error after COMMIT is unknown**:

| Reply to k's final message | Data transaction | k's callers | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Error before COMMIT: ReadyForQuery `E`, ROLLBACK acknowledged | Aborted | Known abort; §3.1 and §4.2 rerun rules | Unchanged |
| COMMIT tag, marker `UPDATE 1` and COMMIT tag, ReadyForQuery `I`, no error or warning | Committed, covering marker confirmed | Confirmed; released in order once D covers k | Raised to k |
| COMMIT tag, then a marker error, `UPDATE 0`, a "canceling wait" warning or no reply | Committed, durability unconfirmed | `E_COMMIT_OUTCOME_UNKNOWN`, **no automatic rerun**: k's attempt is in `_head`, so a rerun applies twice | Unchanged; bump and recover (§3.9) |
| Error at COMMIT, or connection lost, with no COMMIT tag | By `pg_xact_status` after the drain: aborted, or else the row above; unknown if in progress or unanswerable | Known abort only if aborted; otherwise unknown, no rerun | Bump unless aborted |

### 4.2 Drain before recovery, cascades and reruns

G7 removes the opener lock that made a barrier's `_head FOR UPDATE` wait
for old writers, so a dispatched final message can still be parked before
its CAS when recovery reads the head. **Drain rule.** A transaction is
*affected* if its final message was dispatched and no terminal result
(confirmed COMMIT, or error with acknowledged ROLLBACK) has returned. After
a known abort or unknown outcome, nothing is re-seeded and no rerun,
barrier or batch is dispatched until **every affected transaction has
ended**, shown by:

1. its own terminal result; or
2. a **ticket probe**: after its fence, the recovery barrier's opener takes
   each affected batch's ticket, highest revision first, under a bounded
   `lock_timeout`. Each batch took its ticket in P1, so acquiring it proves
   that transaction ended; affected batches wait only on lower ones (§3.5),
   never on a ticket the probe holds, so the probe closes no cycle; or
3. **fenced termination**: if a probe times out, terminate the tagged
   writers, wait until they are gone and reacquire with a fresh token
   (store-port §5.2), as G6's sticky rule already requires.

Only then does the opener take `_head FOR UPDATE` and re-seed seq and
revision (the attempt only rises, §3.6) from a head no old transaction can
move; under E that proves an end, not durability (§3.9). The rule covers
cascades and any number of unknowns, and since **every dispatched successor
is resolved before any rerun is dispatched**, the nonce is defence in depth
against ABA.

**Cascades.** A known abort of k aborts every chain successor. k's own
members follow controls §3.1: remove a named failed member and rerun the
rest in order, or rerun each alone, within its deadline. **Successors are
innocent known aborts**, their facts only conditional on k: each reruns
**once, as a whole batch**, in original order after k's survivors and
**after complete drainage**, with fresh facts, original deadlines, and as
each member's one fallback attempt; a second failure falls to §3.1's solo
rule (ruling 2). All §3.7 state from the aborted chain is discarded, and no
reply precedes a rerun.

### 4.3 Unknown k with k+1 in flight

If k+1 has not been dispatched, it rolls back and the pipeline pauses:
under G6 until fenced reacquisition; under G5 the recovery barrier drains
(§4.2) and reads the real head whichever way k went; under E it also bumps
the epoch and recovers (§3.9). k's members keep `E_COMMIT_OUTCOME_UNKNOWN`;
k+1's rerun within their deadlines.

If k+1 has been dispatched, its connection resolves it, and with the nonce
its evidence about k is unambiguous: a committed k+1 CAS'd against k's
attempt, so k committed; a failed CAS observing k−1's attempt means k ended
uncommitted. Under ruling 3 this **successor witness reconciles internal
state only** (sequencer, diagnostics): k's callers still get unknown,
nothing reruns automatically and the G6 gate stays sticky. On the
synchronous arms a confirmed k+1 is released in order once k's members are
classified (§3.6); under E, §3.9's bump settles it. A dispatched k+1 widens
its own unknown window by the ticket wait, at most one predecessor flush.

### 4.4 Ownership, deadlines and crash recovery

- **Ownership loss.** The K0-Q watchdog latches loss and refuses unstarted
  work. If the lease is lost before a final message, undispatched batches
  roll back and their started members get `E_STORE_OWNERSHIP`; dispatched
  batches deliver their real outcome. Takeover waits for every key share
  and terminates the old token's backends and tickets (store-port §5.2);
  renewal touches neither. An unacknowledged rollback stays unknown
  (`outcome.rs:22–36`); under E, §3.9 adds the old owner's refusal.
- **Deadlines.** `lock_timeout` bounds the ticket wait by the earliest
  member's remaining deadline, a new enforcement point for first attempts
  (`program_batch.rs:239`). A 55P03 is a known abort with COMMIT skipped
  (§3.2), whose members get deadline failures without rerun. COMMIT runs
  with timeouts at zero and is uncancellable (`ownership.rs:141–167`), and
  the combined message is never cancelled to reopen it.
- **Crash.** If the process dies with k and k+1 dispatched, k+1 can commit
  only after T_k is released and `_head` carries k's attempt, so the
  database holds nothing, k, or k then k+1: **never k+1 without k**. Seq
  stays gap-free because head advances only by an exact CAS in the
  transaction holding that batch's `_commits` rows; a "canceling wait"
  warning fails closed. Under E this is a fact about committed WAL; D
  governs what Snapback releases (§3.9).

## 5. Budget: flights, CPU and latency

### 5.1 Stage model

For one batch, X is collection to dispatch of the final message,
`X = K_pre + P1 + interpretation (+ dependent levels)`. C is dispatch to
confirmation, `C = R + w + apply + CAS + COMMIT_server`, plus a further R
before COMMIT in three-flight; C₀ is C without the ticket wait w. The chain
is the server-serialized time from the predecessor's end to the batch's
own: `apply + COMMIT_server (+R three-flight)`, about `COMMIT_server + δ`
with G7.4, and `apply + COMMIT_async` with E. At depth d,
`I = max(X, (X + C₀)/d, chain, CPU)` and `L = H + I_ing + Q + X + C +
release`. When the chain binds, slots fill and k+1 waits at the ticket for
`w = d·I − X − C₀`; otherwise w ≈ 0. Q ≈ I/2 assumes uniform phases
(§5.4). In E1, k+1's P1 waits server-side for k's lock release, so
`I = X + C_vis − R` with `C_vis = R + apply + COMMIT_async`, and
`L = H + I_ing + Q + I + C_vis + F + release`, F being the marker's flush.

Inputs. "Measured" means the §2.1 means, at N≈16.

- R = 0.55 / 1.10 ms (measured, A / B). Interpretation 0.62 / 0.73 ms
  (measured). P1 = facts + 0.15 ms of fused-opener work: 1.94 / 2.38 ms.
- Apply server = apply+tail span − R: 2.11 / 1.68 ms. COMMIT server =
  COMMIT span − R: 2.90 / 2.75 ms; COMMIT_async = that − 2.6 ms sync:
  0.30 / 0.15 ms (inferred). F = 2.6 ms (assumed).
- K = 3.17 / 2.97 ms, split evenly before dispatch and after confirmation
  (inferred); release, the mean wait for a reply after confirmation, is
  about half of K_post: 0.8 ms (assumed).
- H = 0.6 ms, unmeasured on an undelayed Unix socket; I_ing = R + 0.15 ms;
  folded publication +0.1 ms (all assumed).

Nothing is iterated to self-consistency (projected N = rate × I is about
11–14); read every projection as ±15%.

### 5.3 Depth

Depth 2 suffices when C ≤ X plus a little. With the levers (X+C)/2 still
binds; depth 3 buys about 0.5–0.6 ms of L, beyond 3 nothing, each unit
costs a reader, and it needs G7.4 or E to bring the chain below X. **Build
d=3 only if measurements show (X+C)/2 binding** (G7.5).

### 5.4 Verdict on 7.07 ms and 14.15 ms

- **B (14.15 ms) is reachable** with G7.3 and the two levers: projected
  L ≈ 12.9 ms at d=2 (12.4 at d=3 with G7.4 or E), about 88–91% client
  occupancy at 2,262/s. Plausible, not assured; it would flip **W4/B and
  W5 mix/B**. G7.2 alone (≈1.9k) and E1 (≈1.8–2.1k) only tie at 1,600.
- **A (7.07 ms) is forecast as a loss, not shown impossible.** One facts
  flight, one final flight, the request's own sync and half a chain
  interval of queueing cost about 7.8 ms under uniform phases, and E still
  waits for each request's flush. The levered projections (11.7–12.3 ms)
  miss even 3,200; the Supabase advocate's own p50 at 3,200/s is 8.02 ms.
- **Q is the term the model is least sure of.** If collection phase-locks
  to bursty returns (M3's bounded collection policy), or batches stop
  queueing behind each other (arm C), Q falls and the floor's other terms
  total about 6.3 ms; nothing yet shows either is achievable. Pre-register
  **W4/A as a forecast loss under every ordered arm** (ruling 7). W5 mix/A
  (1,131 against 800) and exact W5 on both links already win and must not
  regress.

## 6. SQLite

SQLite does not get G7 or E: its write lock spans `BEGIN IMMEDIATE`
through the WAL fsync, there are no network flights to hide, and at about
7.9 ms per fsync batching (G3) dominates. In-order release and the
sequencer cache run there at d=1, where the chain is empty, so **the
production differential never exercises the fold**; §8.2's held-chain mode
does, on SQLite by holding k's write transaction open while k+1 reads
through a WAL reader.

## 7. Alternatives considered

- **C: concurrent writer transactions** (controls §10.4) and the panel's
  commuting-batch variant. Independent transactions share flushes and need
  no batch queue: S1b's raw durable disjoint control, 5,890/s at C=32,
  implies L ≈ 5.4 ms, the only evaluated route under 7.07 ms on A. The
  variant (disjoint batches commit concurrently with in-process seq ranges,
  head = highest contiguous committed seq, holes voided before readers pass
  them) is **unsound as stated**: `group_tail` updates `r` in place, so a
  non-tip batch is visible at its own commit, before head moves or any void
  exists; voiding an unknown range can bury a committed batch; and `_outbox`
  and `_ops` rows in non-prefix snapshots break G5/G6 revision equality,
  `/changes`, `_change_evidence` and keyed replay. **A repair needs**
  watermark checks on every PG read path (or per-epoch reader gating, or
  versioned rows), voids sharing the `_commits.seq` key and issued only once
  the original provably cannot commit, with explicit semantics, and
  takeover reconciliation of open holes; a per-transaction revision would
  recreate the chain, and a publisher fsync could eat the gain. C
  **complements G7**, since hot keys still serialize. **E's head check on
  every read path is the same requirement** as this repair's watermark and
  weighs the same (§3.8's head-bound transport would serve either); what
  separates them is C's in-place visibility, voids and takeover holes,
  against its one advantage, Q, which only G7.0's arm C prices.
  **Commission it only on measured evidence** (ruling 7).
- **Larger or fuller batches.** Under G7, N self-sizes to rate × I; under
  the serial schedule N=32 idles the writer during H (fill-only forecast
  1.9–2.1k A, 1.5–1.7k B); a coalescing wait adds to Q.
- **`commit_delay`, global `synchronous_commit`, fsync.** `commit_delay`
  merges only concurrent committers' flushes; the synchronous chain has
  none. Global `synchronous_commit` or fsync off is forbidden; arm E differs
  in kind (§3.8). Storage tuning is a matched change, for the author.
- **Rejected:** same-connection pipelining of k+1's P1 behind k's COMMIT
  (it runs only after k's flush); two-phase commit (two flushes per batch);
  a head CAS without a ticket or nonce (§3.4, §4.2); no `_head` row (every
  reader's head statement and G5/G6 stamp changed, unmeasured).

### 8.2 Witnesses

1. **Randomized serial-equivalence differential against N=1**, extending
   G3b's paired oracle (seeds, 192 members, both stores): random batch
   boundaries, d ∈ {1, 2, 3}, adversarial slot-reply order, hot keys and
   keyed duplicates across batches, refusals and barriers, comparing
   outcomes, meters, receipts, rows, sequences, change evidence, log images,
   outbox, head, revision and attempt. **Held-chain mode** holds k's final
   message until k+1 has interpreted, so every batch folds a non-empty
   chain (§6); under injected failures the committer §3.5 oracle checks
   history.
2. **Fault matrix between k and k+1**, with k+1 dispatched and not: apply
   failure in k; CAS mismatch; k terminated before and after COMMIT; socket
   drop after dispatch; ticket timeout (a sleeping deferred trigger);
   adoption between P1 and final; takeover and SIGKILL with both in flight;
   at d=3, k+1's 55P03 while k runs. Under E: a marker error after COMMIT,
   `UPDATE 0`, a reply lost between COMMIT and marker, an error at COMMIT, a
   "canceling wait" warning, a PostgreSQL crash with held readers and
   pending fills, takeover with a marker in flight. Each asserts §4.1, no
   reply before rerun, a contiguous head and exact counters.
3. **Drain and ABA.** (a) k unknown while k+1 is parked before its CAS: the
   barrier must not read `_head` before the T_{k+1} probe succeeds. (b)
   **Past the drain**: a test-only hook disables it, and a rerun of k's
   survivors, forced to leave k's numeric end values, commits while k+1 is
   held before its CAS; k+1 must fail on the attempt alone. (c) No attempt
   repeats under one token across every fault sequence. (d) Two and three
   consecutive unknowns. (e) A probe timeout falls to fenced termination.
4. **Provisional-state rollback.** After every known-abort and unknown case,
   each §3.7 row's digest equals the serial reference's after the last
   released batch.
5. **Transaction status.** After every fault case, `pg_xact_status` of each
   P1 xid matches its classification: confirmed ⇒ committed; known abort
   (55P03 and raised assertions included, proving COMMIT was skipped) ⇒
   aborted; unknown ⇒ either; under E, as E's table in §4.1.
6. **Visibility hammer.** In every concurrent REPEATABLE READ snapshot,
   `_head.seq` is the maximum contiguous `_commits.seq`, a batch's marker
   images exist if and only if its commits do, and `/changes` pages are
   contiguous. G6 hits racing dispatch, confirmation and install never
   serve outside confirmed in-order publication, `read()` observes every
   closure, and unknown closes the gate.
7. **Locks.** Zero `pg_stat_database.deadlocks` delta on one store; waits
   only on tickets, or on a lower batch's `_head` or rows after an early end
   (§3.5), never on disjoint `r` rows; a forced cross-store collision yields
   at most a 40P01 known abort.
8. **RT0 ratchets.** 3 (G7.2) or 2 (G7.3, E1) flights per batch for every
   N; tickets and markers add none; idle N=1 budgets unchanged.
9. **Release order** equals revision order under every completion order.
10. **E only.** An immediate PostgreSQL shutdown under load recovers a
    prefix containing every acknowledged write, and **every reader
    endpoint** (queries, sync expansion, receipt replay, G5 fills, caches,
    claimers, `/changes`) emits nothing above D; a reader whose snapshot saw
    a later-lost revision is discarded when it is reused; `UPDATE 0` never
    raises D; each epoch's first D is its recovery commit's H; no reader
    outlives the hold bound. With the role's session default
    `synchronous_commit=off` all of this still holds; an old owner's late
    marker neither blocks provisioning nor moves the new owner's D.

## 9. Amendments this RFC would make

- **Committer §3.4 and §7.** Pipelined batches use the ticket chain and the
  final message's nonce CAS; barriers keep §3.4 but write a fresh attempt.
  Adaptive sizing counts ticket waits.
- **Controls §3.3, §4.1, §6.** "Under the head lock" becomes "under the
  commit ticket" for pipelined batches; G6 closure becomes §3.6's count;
  under E, G5 linearizes by the D hold and reader rows arrive only as
  head-bound values; §6's G7 row points here.
- **Round-trip §3.5.** Generation and receipt re-checks move after the
  ticket wait; receipt copies resolve through the fold, `_ops` as backstop.
  "COMMIT is its own flight" holds through G7.2; G7.3 adopts §10.7's.
- **Store-port §5, §5.2, §7.2, §8.** Cascade rows and the drain rule;
  `_head` gains `attempt` (under E, plus `_durable` rows per slot and
  token, the unfenced marker's exception to §5, recovery epochs and the
  synchronous recovery commit); d writer slots on their own threads,
  readers 31−d.
- **S1 method §4**, only if E is built and ruling 8 admits it:
  distinguish per-transaction early lock release with acknowledged
  durability from a relaxed `synchronous_commit=off` cell.

## 10. Rulings

The orchestrator decided rulings 1–6 and 7's disposition after round 1;
rounds 2 and 3 changed none of them. Ruling 8 was decided after G7.0 (below).

1. **Connection budget: yes.** The second writer comes from the 30
   readers; W2/A and W1/B are re-measured with 29 readers, 28 at d=3 (§8.3).
2. **Cascade reruns: yes.** One whole-batch fresh-fact rerun of innocent
   successors, in original order, after complete drainage (§4.2).
3. **Successor witness: no, for now.** Successor evidence reconciles
   internal state but neither resolves k's callers nor permits a §3.1 rerun
   (§4.3). Revisit once the nonce CAS has shipped and §8.2 items 3 and 5
   show its evidence is unambiguous. **Dissent (grok):** flip it now: k+1's
   CAS-commit resolves k as committed, a CAS failure observing k's start as
   a known abort, anything else stays sticky-unknown; under "no", an unkeyed
   member's durable write leaves its caller unknown.
4. **Two-flight final (G7.3): yes, conditionally** on controls §10.7's M2
   witnesses, raising assertions, transaction-status witnesses and a
   ticket-wait timeout that provably skips COMMIT (§3.2, §8.2 item 5).
5. **G6 hit wait: yes.** A short bound measured on mixed workloads, then G5.
6. **Writer driver: yes.** Per-slot synchronous threads, off PIPE (§3.10).
7. **W4/A: a forecast loss.** G7.0
   ([results](../snapback4/experiments/postgres-commit-path/results-20261025-g70/README.md))
   puts S at 8.6–8.7 ms and E2 at 7.01–7.03 ms against 7.07 ms in a
   SQL-only harness; E2's figure is saturation, not headroom, and the
   product adds HTTP intake, authority and committer CPU. Arm C (6.93 ms)
   needs the unbuilt visibility layer (§7) and is not commissioned.
8. **Early lock release: admitted, with conditions (decided 2026-10-25
   after a blind three-model panel: two option (a), one option (d); all
   three: E preserves acknowledged-write durability).** Arm E (§3.8–§3.9)
   is Snapback's product behaviour and may be a disclosed headline S1
   configuration. S1 method §4 is amended, before any arm-E product
   result, to define durability by acknowledgement
   ([LLP 3000.009.001.002](./3000.009.001.002-scale-limits-against-postgres-controls.rfc.md)
   §4). Conditions: (i) a per-acknowledgement flush-position witness in
   qualification (the batch's commit LSN ≤ the flushed LSN when its reply
   is sent), plus a power-cut style crash test where the host permits it
   (SIGKILL keeps the page cache); (ii) the head-bound reader transport
   covers every read path, including SQL reads and `ctx.sql` in actions;
   (iii) any E headline carries the read cells re-run under concurrent
   writes; (iv) the agent-facing deploy guide states the raw-SQL
   visibility window. **Build order:** E (as G7.4's replacement) is built
   after G7.2 lands, in parallel with G7.3 and the levers, because G7.0's
   measured B slack for S (≈0.7 ms) is consumed by measured HTTP intake
   (0.65–0.95 ms); not gated on a B miss. Dissent (one panelist): build
   G7.3 + levers first and build E only if B misses in the product.

G7.0 answers without a ruling: the K/H split; whether N=1 joins a
non-empty pipeline; and whether E's markers and C's commits merge flushes in
practice, with arm C's L once the fence and tail are on each transaction.
