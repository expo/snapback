## 1. Summary

On the one-host S1 runner Snapback's group committer runs one batch at a
time. A batch averages N≈16 of its 32-member cap, makes five (G5) or six
(G6) serial PG flights and pays a ~2.6 ms durable WAL sync before the next
batch may begin. That caps W4 near 1.1k writes/s, and the final comparison
qualifies 800/s on both links against the Supabase advocate's 3,200 (A) and
1,600 (B), which roughly ten independent backends sharing PostgreSQL's
group commit achieve. Strictly beating the controls needs the next rungs:
4,525/s on A and 2,262/s on B.

This RFC designs **G7, a pipelined group commit for the Postgres store**.
While batch k's final message (apply, tail, COMMIT) is in flight and its WAL
flush is running, batch k+1 is collected, fetches its facts and executes on
a second writer connection, reading through k's frozen, uncommitted overlay.
k+1's commit is conditional on k through a **commit ticket**, a store-keyed
transaction advisory lock that k takes in its first flight and k+1 waits on
in its final message, followed by an exact **compare-and-set of `_head`**
against the values and **per-attempt nonce** k leaves. Recovery **drains**
every dispatched transaction before it re-seeds or reruns. Only buffered
G3b waves pipeline; every other producer is a **barrier** that drains the
pipeline and runs today's protocol. Results, receipts, continuations and
publication are released strictly in revision order.

Three findings shape what this design can promise:

1. **Synchronously committed ordered batches cannot share a flush.** A
   synchronous commit releases locks only after its flush, so whatever makes
   k+1 wait for k wakes it after k's flush: one sync per batch (§2.3). Until
   G7.4 the chain is apply plus COMMIT, 5.0–5.6 ms on A, and it binds there
   (§5.2); only at about one sync (≈5.7k/s at N=16) does the writer stop
   limiting throughput.
2. **Early lock release lifts that limit, at an admissibility cost.** k
   commits with `synchronous_commit=off` and a same-message durable marker:
   locks release before the flush, nothing is acknowledged until a durable
   flush covers it, and in-process readers hold output until it covers their
   snapshot. Its depth-1 form needs no speculation. The S1 method fixes
   `synchronous_commit`, so arm E needs ruling 8 (§3.8).
3. **With 32 closed-loop clients, latency binds.** By Little's law the rate
   cannot exceed 32/L, L being mean end-to-end write latency: L ≤ 7.07 ms on
   A and ≤ 14.15 ms on B (§2.2). A pipeline removes the wait for the
   previous batch, not a request's own flights, sync or queueing.

Planning arithmetic, not measurement (§5): G7.2 roughly doubles W4 to about
2.0k (A) and 1.9k (B); E1, E's depth-1 form, projects 1.8–2.1k. With the
two-flight final, G7.4 or E, and two levers named elsewhere
(non-authorizing `/m` ingress, committer CPU near 100 µs/member), B reaches
about 2.5–2.6k, making **the B rungs (W4/B, W5 mix/B at 2,262)
plausible**, and A about 2.6–2.7k. The modelled floor under uniform phases,
about 7.8 ms, exceeds A's bound, so **W4/A is a forecast loss under every
ordered arm**, not an impossibility (§5.4); G7.0 measures arms S, E and C
before any visibility-layer project (ruling 7).

**Decision proposed:** build in §8.1's order: the cold-reader convoy fix
(lane R1, elsewhere) first, since no gain qualifies while first native
repetitions fail counter equality; G7.0 (arms S/E/C, K/H split); the two
levers; G7.1 (fused opener, store-keyed ticket, nonce CAS: 5/6 flights to
3); then E1 and G7.2/G7.3 with E replacing G7.4 if ruling 8 admits E, else
G7.2–G7.4. Rulings 1–6 are decided, 7 waits on G7.0, 8 is open (§10).

Citations use paths relative to `snapback4/` at `25ee876966`. Bare names

## 2. The measured problem

### 2.1 One batch at a time

The diagnosis traced S1 writes at offered 1,131/s. The table shows mean wall
time per batch under G6 from its write-attribution table. "Residual" is the
cycle minus timed phases: the two opener flights and untimed committer CPU.

| Phase | W4-A ms | W4-B ms | Flights |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Residual: opener + untimed committer work | 4.47 | 5.37 | 2 |
| Facts: composed authority, receipts, level 0 (`batch.rs:64–84`) | 1.79 | 2.23 | 1 |
| Parallel interpretation (G3b-par) | 0.62 | 0.73 | 0 |
| Apply + vector tail (`group_tail.rs:6–45`) | 2.66 | 2.78 | 1 |
| G6 publication witness (`read_grant.rs:64`, `store.rs:744`) | 0.75 | 1.24 | 1 |
| COMMIT, including ≈2.6 ms WAL sync | 3.45 | 3.85 | 1 |
| **Cycle** (mean N 15.67 / 15.98) | **13.73** | **16.19** | **6** |

A PG flight costs R ≈ 0.55 ms on A and 1.10 ms on B (the diagnosis prices
two saved flights at 1.1/2.2 ms). Charging the opener flights at R+0.1 ms
each leaves **K ≈ 3.1 ms per batch of unattributed committer CPU**, about
195 µs/member: prepare, bind, closure, finish and acknowledgement. G3b-par
loopback measured 99.5 µs/member at N≈32 without HTTP. K is inferred from
means the diagnosis warns are not additive; G7.0 measures it (§8).

Two complementary groups of about 16 clients alternate, each waiting for
the other's cycle and then its own (≈987/s on B). Collection is microseconds
and conflict reruns are zero, so the serial schedule sets the rate.

### 2.2 What 7.07 ms and 14.15 ms mean

S1 drives 32 write clients, each with at most one request outstanding, so
**the sustained rate is at most 32/L**, L being the mean time from send to
response. 4,525/s needs L ≤ 7.07 ms (A); 2,262/s needs L ≤ 14.15 ms (B).
These are means with no allowance for the 100 ms p99; at about 90% client
occupancy they become 6.4 and 12.7 ms. Under any schedule,

`L = H + I_ing + Q + (batch execution) + (commit) + (release)`

H is HTTP and generator time outside the server. I_ing is the warm
authority statement `ingress_of` (`crates/snapback4/src/serve_identity.rs:8`)
makes per mutation, outside the batch. Q is the wait to be collected. A
pipeline changes how these compose; it cannot relax 32/L.

### 2.3 What a pipeline removes, and what it cannot

Today Q is about one cycle. A pipeline cuts Q to about half a batch interval
and overlaps one batch's flights and CPU with another's flush: the whole
gain. What it cannot do, **while every batch commits synchronously**, is
merge flushes. A synchronous commit inserts the commit record, flushes WAL
to it, marks the transaction visible and only then releases its locks, so
whatever makes k+1 wait for k (a row lock, an advisory lock, the head tuple)
wakes it after k's flush, and k+1's record needs a later one. If k+1 commits
without waiting, it can become durable and visible while k aborts, a gap
below head; two-phase commit adds a flush (§7). Flushes are shared only by
transactions that do not wait on each other (option C, §7) or by ordered
batches whose locks release before their flush (arm E, §3.8).

So **the synchronous chain carries one serialized WAL sync per batch**.
With G7.4 its capacity, N/(sync+δ), is about 5.7k/s at N=16; before G7.4
it also carries apply and binds on A (§5.2). Every request still pays its
own facts flight, final flight and sync, plus queueing, which §5 models as
Q ≈ I/2 for uniformly phased returns. That is a model, not a bound:
closed-loop clients can return in bursts.

## 3. Design

### 3.1 Shape, eligibility and barriers

- **Depth.** At most d batches are in flight, from first flight until
  release. The baseline is **d = 2**; **d = 3** is conditional on §5.3.
- **Pipelined batches.** Only buffered G3b waves (`program_batch.rs:268–273`)
  whose members the bounded closure selects: existing rows, unchanged index
  keys, no inserts, deletes, allocations, maintained or capped writes, and
  no time- or presence-dependent programs (controls §6, G3b note). The
  physical write set lies within those rows' `r` images, the tail tables
  (`_commits`, `_change_evidence`, `_log`, `_outbox`, `_effect_presence`),
  `_ops` and `_head`. A late unknown family that would take G3b's serial
  recovery aborts the attempt before dispatch and reruns as a barrier.
- **Barriers.** Every other producer: Engine, Publication, Direct,
  administrative and long members, non-buffered program groups, activation
  and adoption. A barrier waits until every in-flight batch is released,
  then runs at depth 1 through today's opener (`store.rs:314–322`, `:359`,
  with `_head FOR UPDATE`) and tail, which also writes a fresh head nonce
  (§3.4). Session mints and revocations are Direct members, so **authority
  changes are always barriers**.
- **N = 1 and collection.** An idle lone member keeps today's solo route
  (`program_batch.rs:55–57`); behind a non-empty pipeline it pipelines as a
  one-member wave. Collection is unchanged (`command_batch.rs:206–237`). A
  batch starts once its predecessor's final message is out and a slot frees.

### 3.2 The flights of a pipelined batch

**P1, one message** (replacing today's BEGIN+fence, head/generation/
authority and facts flights):

1. `BEGIN ISOLATION LEVEL READ COMMITTED READ WRITE` and the settings guard
   (`flight.rs:35–61`).
2. The owner fence, `SELECT store_id, token FROM _owner … FOR KEY SHARE`,
   as the first command (`store.rs:183`).
3. **The ticket**: `pg_advisory_xact_lock(t(store, k mod 64))` (§3.4).
4. Generation and `_head` (seq, revision, attempt), read **without** a lock
   as the authority SQL already does (`batch.rs:28`), and
   `pg_current_xact_id()` for transaction-status witnesses (§8.2).
5. The composed authority, receipt/placement and level-0 statements
   (`batch.rs:18–41`, `:64–84`). Authority is read once.

The fence check stays client-side; every later command is a lock or a read,
so a refused fence leaves nothing behind (round-trip §3.5). Dependent levels
remain composed level flights. **CPU** then binds, folds the chain (§3.3),
interprets in parallel, accepts in revision order and builds the tail.

**Final message.** In G7.2, three-flight, one message, then COMMIT:

1. `SET LOCAL statement_timeout` and `lock_timeout` to the earliest
   member's remaining deadline (§4.4).
2. Wait on the predecessor's ticket.
3. The nonce CAS (§3.4) and a generation and identity check that also
   captures publication, each its own command after the wait, so each takes
   a fresh READ COMMITTED snapshot (round-trip §3.5).
4. Bulk apply plus the vector tail, with the existing counts
   (`group_tail.rs:222–227`).

The client verifies the counts, then sends `SET LOCAL statement_timeout=0;
SET LOCAL lock_timeout=0; COMMIT` alone (`store.rs:746–756`). In G7.3,
two-flight, the same message continues with raising count assertions and
that reset plus COMMIT: controls §10.7's qualified variant. A 55P03 at the
ticket wait, a failed CAS or a raised assertion leaves the transaction
failed; PostgreSQL skips the rest of the message, COMMIT included, and the
client's ROLLBACK is acknowledged, the skip ruling 4 requires (§8.2 item
5). That is **3 flights per batch (G7.2) or 2 (G7.3), independent of N**,
against 5 (G5) and 6 (G6) today; the ticket wait is not a flight.

### 3.3 Reading through uncommitted predecessors

When batch j has accepted its last member, the committer freezes a
**pending overlay P_j**: the final image of every row j writes (the
`accepted` map, `wave.rs:293–301`); every receipt and refusal receipt j
records, keyed by `(principal, id)`, with its complete binding and outcome;
the `_head` (seq, revision, attempt) j will leave; and §3.7's provisional
state after j's last member.

The **chain** of batch k is the set of batches not yet released when k's P1
is dispatched, at most d−1. Before k interprets, the committer **folds** it,
in revision order, into k's batch base and receipt facts: chain images and
receipts replace fetched ones, and fetching a covered key may be skipped.
The existing lookup (own writes, accepted predecessors, base;
`overlay.rs:71–100`) and the wave's conflict detection (`wave.rs:262–292`)
run unchanged.

**Why the fold is exact.** Between barriers, under the fence, only
pipelined batches write, so any snapshot P1 takes sees the committed state
after some prefix of k's chain. A key a chain member writes gets its latest
pending value from the fold; every committed state in that range agrees on
other keys. The folded base is therefore the state after k−1, however the
commits interleave with P1, and the controls RFC's induction (§3.3, "Why
serial-equivalent") extends verbatim: k's members are members N+1, N+2, …
of a merged batch k−1 ∪ k, with the rules, refusals, meters, images,
receipts, effective times and sequence positions of serial reference `run`.

The first slice relies on G3b's selector: no absence, range or index entry
can change, so the fold is a pure replacement and `covers_span`
(`wave.rs:274`) is unaffected. A later family joins only after its fold
rule for absence, ranges and index entries qualifies under the same
differential.

**Authority stays fresh.** Program members cannot write session, identity,
generation or `_meta` state (those producers are barriers), so k's P1
authority statement is the final authority read inside k's fenced
transaction (round-trip §3.1, §7). Authority deadlines are checked at
admission (committer §3.6) and again just before the final message. Pending
images stay within each batch's 32 MiB fact reservation.

### 3.4 The commit ticket and the nonce CAS

**Why `_head FOR UPDATE` must leave the opener.** Every writer takes it
first today (`store.rs:315`, `:359`), so k+1's P1 would wait for k's COMMIT:
the serial schedule. **A head CAS alone is not enough**: two connections are
not ordered on the server, so k+1's final message can reach `_head` before
k's tail updates it (`group_tail.rs:7`), failing spuriously, or lock a row k
is about to update.

**The ticket.** Each batch takes a transaction advisory lock T_k in P1. It
writes no WAL and is released only when the transaction ends, by commit,
abort or termination. k+1's final message is never dispatched before k's P1
returns, because k+1's interpretation needs P_k, so k holds T_k before k+1
waits on it, and k+1 proceeds only after k has ended. **Keys derive from
store identity**: one 64-bit key whose high 58 bits come from
`_meta.store_id` (32 hex digits) and whose low 6 bits are `k mod 64`. Slots
of one store never collide. Stores sharing a database collide only if their
ids share those bits, which can add a wait or a cross-store cycle (§3.5),
never a wrong commit. Slot reuse is safe: batch k+64 cannot start until k
has ended (d ≤ 3).

**The CAS.** `_head` gains an **`attempt`** pair: the owner token and a
per-ownership counter. Tokens rise on every acquisition
(`ownership.rs:313–335`), so no value repeats. Every `_head` writer,
barriers and reruns included, sets a fresh attempt. k+1 runs `UPDATE _head
SET seq=$s1, revision=$r1, attempt=$a1 WHERE singleton AND seq=$s0 AND
revision=$r0 AND attempt=$a0 RETURNING …` with k's leaving values. As a
fresh statement after the wait, it matches **if and only if k's own attempt
committed**; a rerun or replacement with equal numeric end values cannot
satisfy it (the ABA case of §4.2). Zero rows aborts k+1, by raising in G7.3
or by the client's count check in G7.2, reporting the observed head (§4.3).
The CAS replaces the seq-only compare (`group_tail.rs:7`) and the relative
`revision = revision + n` (`revision.rs:80–101`).

### 3.5 Other serializing rows, and deadlock

| Row or table | Today | Under G7 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `_head` | `FOR UPDATE` at open; seq CAS and revision bump in the tail | Unlocked read in P1; one nonce CAS after the ticket wait; never contended |
| `_change_evidence` prune | `DELETE … WHERE seq <= last−1000` (`group_tail.rs:22`); ranges overlap | `seq > s0−1000 AND seq <= s1−1000`: disjoint across in-flight batches |
| `_commits`, `_log`, `_outbox`, `_effect_presence` | Inserts | Unchanged; seq ranges are disjoint per batch |
| `_ops` | Insert of the bound receipt | Duplicates across batches resolve through the fold; a key collision is a 23505 known abort |
| `r` images | Bulk update of final images | A row both k and k+1 write is updated by k+1 only after the ticket wait |
| `_owner` | Key share; renewal `FOR NO KEY UPDATE` | Unchanged and compatible (store-port §5.1) |
| `_meta`, `_sequences`, maintained owner rows, cap rows | Written by some families | Not written by first-slice members; their writers stay barriers |

**Within one store the wait graph is acyclic.** Before its ticket wait a
pipelined batch holds only the fence's key share and its own ticket; after
it, every lower batch's locks are gone. Waits point only at the immediate
predecessor, and recovery probes take tickets highest first (§4.2). G7.4's
**pre-wait disjoint apply** writes, before the wait, only rows provably
absent from every chain member's write set, shrinking the chain to roughly
the sync without touching any row an in-flight batch writes.

**Across stores it is not.** A ticket-key collision between stores sharing
a database, or a foreign advisory-lock user, can close a cycle. PostgreSQL's
detector then aborts one transaction with 40P01, which the commit path
already classifies as a known abort (`store.rs:719–724`). **Nothing here
claims 40P01 cannot occur.** The witnesses are a zero
`pg_stat_database.deadlocks` delta on one store and a forced collision
(§8.2).

### 3.6 Sequencer, in-order release and the publication gate

**The sequencer.** The committer assigns each batch its seq range, revision
range and attempt in memory, from its predecessor's end. The CAS'd head is
the durable truth; the in-memory values are a cache, like the `_sequences`
counters (committer §4.5), re-seeded from `_head` at every barrier and,
after an abort or unknown outcome, **only after drainage** (§4.2).

**In-order release.** Confirmations can arrive out of order on two sockets.
Continuations, replies, `committed` and `/changes` wakeups, G5 installs,
`BatchStats` and published state are released **strictly in revision
order**. k+1's confirmation waits until k is **resolved**: every member of
k has its final classification, and unknown is one (§4.3). In the database
visibility order is revision order, because k becomes visible before it
releases T_k, so readers, `partition::stream` and effect claimers only ever
see contiguous prefixes.

**The G6 gate.** Today closure is the state write lock held across one
COMMIT (`read_grant.rs:189–240`), which `read()` sees as a failed
`try_read` (`read_grant.rs:147–181`). Two slot threads cannot share that
guard, so closure becomes an **`inflight` count that `read()` checks**:

- **Close before dispatch.** Before any final message, the committer takes
  the write lock, which waits for in-progress hits to drop their read
  guards (today's synchronized admission), increments `inflight`, releases.
- **Readers see it.** Under its read guard, `read()` treats `inflight > 0`
  as closed: it waits up to ruling 5's short bound, then returns `None` and
  the read takes G5.
- **Install in order, then reopen.** On each resolution the committer takes
  the write lock, installs in revision order every confirmed publication
  whose lower batches are resolved (under E, also durable), and decrements
  for each resolved batch. It reopens only at `inflight = 0` with nothing
  uncertain.
- **Sticky uncertainty.** An unknown outcome sets `uncertain`, clears epoch
  and `published` and pauses writer admission until fenced reacquisition
  with a fresh epoch (`read_grant.rs:222–227`). A known abort decrements
  without installing; a panic sets `uncertain`, as today.

Publication capture moves into the final message's identity check,
replacing the `PUBLICATION` query (`store.rs:744`). Under write saturation
the gate is nearly always closed (today 23–24% of G6 write time), but G6's
revision-equality entries already miss under sustained writes; ruling 5
replaces the one-second hit wait. S1's write cells have no G6 hits. **Adaptive
N** (`command_batch.rs:172–203`) keeps its p99 controller, ticket waits
included.

### 3.8 Arm E: early lock release (needs ruling 8)

§2.3's finding holds only for synchronous commits. Here k's locks and
ticket release when its commit record is inserted, before the flush, and
durability is established separately. k's final message ends `SET LOCAL
synchronous_commit=off; SET LOCAL statement_timeout=0; SET LOCAL
lock_timeout=0; COMMIT; UPDATE _durable SET n = n + 1 WHERE slot = $s`.
The last statement is a **durable marker**: its own implicit transaction at
the session's `synchronous_commit=on`, one row per slot. The message
returns only once the WAL flush, and any synchronous standby's, covers the
marker's commit record, which follows k's. It adds no flight; an error
before COMMIT skips the marker too. Barriers keep synchronous commit.

**The durable revision D.** WAL is one ordered log and k+1 commits only
after k's commit record is inserted, so a flush covering k+1 covers k. D
advances to j when j's marker returns, or when a later batch's marker
returns on a connection open continuously since before j's final message
was dispatched (a server restart ends every connection). D therefore
advances in revision order.

**Release and reader hold.** Replies, continuations, receipts, G5 installs,
G6 publication and `/changes` wakeups for k are released only once D ≥ k,
in order. Every in-process PG reader holds output from a snapshot whose head
may exceed D until D covers it: **G5 fills and hits, reader-pool queries,
keyed receipt replay from `_ops`, outbox and effect claimers (which claim
only `seq ≤ D`), `partition::stream` and `/changes`**. A statement that
captures `_head` holds until D reaches that revision; one that does not
holds until D reaches the highest revision dispatched when its result
arrived, which bounds its snapshot. This costs up to one flush of read
latency under writes, which S1's mixed cells must price, and it replaces
controls §4.1's argument that a G5 hit linearizes because
`synchronous_commit=on`.

**Crash and loss.** Recovery replays a WAL prefix, so the head stays
contiguous, and no reply precedes a covering flush, so **no acknowledged
write can be lost**. A server crash can lose an unacknowledged suffix whose
callers, like a lost COMMIT reply's today, get `E_COMMIT_OUTCOME_UNKNOWN`
and resolve keyed retries through receipts. What changes is visibility: **a
raw PostgreSQL client outside Snapback can see rows a crash later loses**;
Snapback's own readers cannot.

**Depth-1 form, E1.** k+1's P1 goes out on the second slot as soon as k's
final message does, with G7.1's fused opener but **today's `_head FOR
UPDATE`** after the fence. That wait wakes at k's lock release, not after
k's flush, and k+1 reads committed state: **no pending overlay, fold,
ticket wait, cascade or successor witness**. If k aborts, k+1 reads the head
before k. If k is unknown, k+1's lock wait proves k's backend ended: §4.2's
drain for free. Under G6 an unknown is still sticky and k+1's undispatched
final rolls back. The nonce CAS stays as the head update. Two slots suffice
while C plus the marker's flush is shorter than the interval (§5.2).

**With the chain.** At d ≥ 2 with the fold, E shortens the chain to apply
plus an asynchronous commit, about 2.4 / 1.8 ms. That is G7.4's purpose
without its overlap-set computation, so **E replaces G7.4**.

**Admissibility.** S1 method §4 fixes `synchronous_commit` on and labels a
`synchronous_commit=off` cell a relaxed row. E keeps `postgresql.conf` and
acknowledged durability, but sets the knob per transaction and exposes
non-durable rows to raw clients. Whether E is a headline cell, and whether
every control's advocate may use it, is ruling 8.

### 3.9 Writer slots, driver and the connection budget

- **Slots.** d writer connections, each tagged `sb:<store>:<holder>:<token>`
  as today, so takeover terminates all of them (store-port §5.2). Under the
  matched cap of 32: 1 owner/lease + d writers + (31−d) readers, **29
  readers at d=2 or E1, 28 at d=3**, against store-port §8's 30 (ruling 1).
- **Driver.** Each slot keeps the synchronous `postgres` client on its own
  thread (ruling 6); no writer joins the PIPE runtime. The committer thread
  never blocks on a socket, and alone owns `&mut Engine` for
  interpretation, acceptance and continuations. Today's `Tx<'a>` borrows the
  store mutably (`store.rs:401–429`); slot transactions become owned and
  `Send`.
- **CPU.** Committer busy time per batch must stay below the interval; it
  is 60–80% at the projections, so K binds next at d=3 (§5.2).

## 4. Outcomes and faults

### 4.1 Outcome table

Controls §3.1, the store-port §5.2 rows and §10.7's two-flight table apply
to each batch on its own connection. G7 adds cross-batch rows. In E1, k+1's
P1 waits for k to end, so a known abort of k needs no rollback of k+1 and
an unknown k follows the first column's G6/G5 rule (§3.8).

| k's outcome | Successor k+1 not yet dispatched | Successor k+1 dispatched (waiting on T_k, or later) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmed | k+1 proceeds | k+1 is classified on its own connection |
| Known abort (explicit error, acknowledged ROLLBACK) | Roll k+1 back: a known abort with no failed member | T_k is released; k+1's CAS finds k−1's attempt and raises; COMMIT is skipped and ROLLBACK acknowledged: a known abort. Drain before any rerun (§4.2) |
| Unknown (lost reply, timeout, terminated during COMMIT, unacknowledged rollback) | Roll k+1 back (known); drain; re-seed (§4.3) | k+1 resolves on its own connection; its evidence reconciles internal state, not k's callers (ruling 3) |
| Ownership lost | Do not dispatch; roll back; `E_STORE_OWNERSHIP` | Actual result; the fence orders it before takeover; termination after dispatch is unknown |

### 4.2 Drain before recovery, cascades and reruns

G7 removes the opener lock that made a barrier's `_head FOR UPDATE` wait
for old writers: a dispatched final message can still be parked before its
CAS when recovery reads the head. **Drain rule.** An *affected* transaction
is one whose final message was dispatched and whose connection has returned
no terminal result (confirmed COMMIT, or error with acknowledged ROLLBACK).
After a known abort or unknown outcome, nothing is re-seeded and no rerun,
barrier or batch is dispatched until **every affected transaction has
ended**, shown by:

1. its own terminal result; or
2. a **ticket probe**: after its fence, the recovery barrier's opener takes
   each affected batch's ticket, highest revision first, under a bounded
   `lock_timeout`. Each batch took its ticket in P1, which returned before
   its final message went out, so acquiring the ticket proves that
   transaction ended. An affected batch waits only on its predecessor's
   ticket, never one the probe holds, so the probe cannot close a cycle; or
3. **fenced termination**: if a probe times out, terminate the tagged
   writers, wait until they are gone and reacquire with a fresh token
   (store-port §5.2), as G6's sticky rule already requires.

Only then does the opener take `_head FOR UPDATE` and re-seed from (seq,
revision, attempt), a head no old transaction can move. A batch whose final
message never went out cannot commit and only rolls back. The rule covers
cascades and any number of unknowns, and since **every dispatched successor
is resolved before any rerun is dispatched**, the nonce is defence in depth
against ABA, not its only guard.

**Cascades.** A known abort of k aborts every chain successor. k's own
members follow controls §3.1: remove a named failed member and rerun the
rest in order, or rerun each alone, within its deadline. **Successors are
innocent known aborts**: no member is implicated, and their facts were only
conditional on k. Each reruns **once, as a whole batch**, in original order
after k's survivors and **after complete drainage**, with fresh facts
against the survivors' committed state, original deadlines, and as each
member's one fallback attempt; a second failure falls to §3.1's solo rule
(ruling 2). All §3.7 state from the aborted chain is discarded. No reply
precedes a rerun.

### 4.3 Unknown k with k+1 in flight

If k+1 has not been dispatched, it rolls back and the pipeline pauses:
under G6 until fenced reacquisition; under G5 the recovery barrier drains
(§4.2), proving k's backend ended, and reads the real head whichever way k
went. k's members keep `E_COMMIT_OUTCOME_UNKNOWN`; k+1's members rerun
within their deadlines.

If k+1 has been dispatched, its connection resolves it, and with the nonce
its evidence about k is unambiguous: a committed k+1 CAS'd against k's
attempt, so k committed; a failed CAS observing k−1's attempt means T_k was
released and the head did not move, so k ended uncommitted. Under ruling 3
this **successor witness reconciles internal state only** (sequencer,
diagnostics): k's callers still get unknown, nothing reruns automatically
and the G6 gate stays sticky. A confirmed k+1 is still released, in order,
once k's members are classified (§3.6). A dispatched k+1 widens its own
unknown window by the ticket wait, at most one predecessor flush.

### 4.4 Ownership, deadlines and crash recovery

- **Ownership loss.** The K0-Q watchdog latches loss and refuses unstarted
  work. If the lease is lost before a final message, undispatched batches
  roll back and their started members get `E_STORE_OWNERSHIP`; dispatched
  batches deliver their real outcome. Takeover's `FOR UPDATE` on `_owner`
  waits for every key share and terminates the old token's backends, and
  their tickets (store-port §5.2). Renewal never touches the head or
  tickets. An unacknowledged rollback stays unknown (`outcome.rs:22–36`).
- **Deadlines.** `lock_timeout` bounds the ticket wait by the earliest
  member's remaining deadline, a new enforcement point for first attempts
  (`program_batch.rs:239`). A 55P03 is a known abort with COMMIT skipped
  (§3.2), whose members get deadline failures without rerun. COMMIT runs
  with timeouts at zero and is uncancellable (`ownership.rs:141–167`); the
  combined message is never cancelled to reopen it. Pre-dispatch checks are
  unchanged (`program_batch.rs:457–494`).
- **Crash.** If the process dies with k and k+1 dispatched, k+1 can commit
  only after T_k is released and `_head` carries k's attempt, so the
  database holds nothing, k, or k then k+1: **never k+1 without k**. Seq
  stays gap-free because head advances only by an exact CAS in the
  transaction holding that batch's `_commits` rows; a new owner reads
  `_head` and continues. A synchronous standby's "canceling wait" warning
  still fails closed. E's crash argument is in §3.8.

## 5. Budget: flights, CPU and latency

### 5.1 Stage model

For one batch, X is collection to dispatch of the final message,
`X = K_pre + P1 + interpretation (+ dependent levels)`. C is dispatch to
confirmation, `C = R + w + apply + CAS + COMMIT_server`, plus a further R
before COMMIT in three-flight; C₀ is C without the ticket wait w. The chain
is the server-serialized time from the predecessor's end to the batch's
own: `apply + COMMIT_server (+R three-flight)`, about `COMMIT_server + δ`
with G7.4, and `apply + COMMIT_async` with E. At depth d,
`I = max(X, (X + C₀)/d, chain, CPU)` and `L = H + I_ing + Q + X + C +
release`. When the chain binds, slots fill and k+1 waits at the ticket for
`w = d·I − X − C₀`; otherwise w ≈ 0. Q ≈ I/2 assumes uniform phases
(§5.4). In E1, k+1's P1 waits server-side for k's lock release, so
`I = X + C_vis − R` with `C_vis = R + apply + COMMIT_async`, and
`L = H + I_ing + Q + I + C_vis + D + release`, D being the marker's flush.

Inputs. "Measured" means the §2.1 means, at N≈16.

- R = 0.55 / 1.10 ms (measured, A / B). Interpretation 0.62 / 0.73 ms
  (measured). P1 = facts + 0.15 ms of fused-opener work: 1.94 / 2.38 ms.
- Apply server = apply+tail span − R: 2.11 / 1.68 ms. COMMIT server =
  COMMIT span − R: 2.90 / 2.75 ms; COMMIT_async = that − 2.6 ms sync:
  0.30 / 0.15 ms (inferred). D = 2.6 ms (assumed).
- K = 3.17 / 2.97 ms, split evenly before dispatch and after confirmation
  (inferred); release, the mean wait for a reply after confirmation, is
  about half of K_post: 0.8 ms (assumed).
- H = 0.6 ms, unmeasured on an undelayed Unix socket; I_ing = R + 0.15 ms;
  folded publication +0.1 ms (all assumed).

Nothing is iterated to self-consistency; projected N = rate × I is about
11–14, so the per-member parts of apply and K shrink somewhat. Read every
projection as ±15%.

### 5.4 Verdict on 7.07 ms and 14.15 ms

- **B (14.15 ms) is reachable** with G7.3, G7.4 or E, and the two levers:
  projected L ≈ 12.4–12.9 ms, about 88–91% client occupancy at 2,262/s.
  Plausible, not assured; it would flip **W4/B and W5 mix/B**. G7.2 alone
  (≈1.9k) and E1 (≈1.8–2.1k) pass 1,600, which ties but does not beat.
- **A (7.07 ms) is forecast as a loss, not shown impossible.** Under
  uniform phases, one facts flight, one final flight, the request's own sync
  and half a chain interval of queueing cost about 7.8 ms with all else
  near zero, and E still waits for each request's flush. The levered
  projections (11.7–12.3 ms) miss even 3,200; the Supabase advocate's own
  p50 at its passing 3,200/s is 8.02 ms, above the 7.07 ms 4,525 needs.
- **Q is the term the model is least sure of.** If collection phase-locks
  to bursty returns (M3's bounded collection policy), or batches stop
  queueing behind each other (arm C), Q falls and the floor's other terms
  total about 6.3 ms; nothing yet shows either is achievable. Pre-register
  **W4/A as a forecast loss under every ordered arm**; G7.0's arm C decides
  on any visibility-layer project (ruling 7). W5 mix/A (1,131 against 800)
  and exact W5 on both links already win and must not regress.

## 6. SQLite

SQLite does not get G7 or E. Its write lock is held from `BEGIN IMMEDIATE`
through the WAL fsync, so a second writer cannot apply while k flushes, and
there are no network flights to hide. At about 7.9 ms per fsync and a
118–122/s engine plateau, batching (G3) dominates, and G5 is disabled.

In-order release and the sequencer cache run at d=1 on SQLite, but at d=1
the chain is always empty, so **the production differential never exercises
the fold on either store**. §8.2's held-chain mode does: it forces non-empty
chains on PG, and on SQLite it keeps k's write transaction open while k+1's
facts are read through a WAL reader and folded.

## 7. Alternatives considered

- **C: concurrent writer transactions** (controls §10.4) and the panel's
  commuting-batch variant. Independent transactions share flushes and need
  no batch queue: S1b's raw durable disjoint control, 5,890/s at C=32,
  implies L ≈ 5.4 ms, the only evaluated route under 7.07 ms on A. The
  variant (disjoint batches commit concurrently with in-process seq ranges,
  head = highest contiguous committed seq, holes voided before readers pass
  them) is **unsound as stated**. `group_tail` updates `r` in place, so a
  non-tip batch is visible at its own commit, before head moves or any void
  exists, and no void can hide it or restore the prior image. Voiding an
  unknown range can bury a committed batch. `_outbox` and `_ops` rows become
  claimable or readable in non-prefix snapshots, breaking G5/G6 revision
  equality, `/changes`, `_change_evidence` and keyed replay even when
  counters balance. **A repair needs** watermark checks on every PG read
  path, including ordinary reads, G5 fills, receipt replay, effect claimers
  and `partition::stream` (or per-epoch reader gating, or versioned rows);
  voids sharing the `_commits.seq` key with the original, issued only once
  it provably cannot commit, with explicit semantics (neither mutation nor
  receipt); and takeover reconciliation of open holes. A per-transaction
  singleton revision would recreate the chain, and a publisher fsync could
  eat the gain. C is simpler to write and much harder to read and recover;
  it **complements G7 rather than subsuming it**, since hot keys still
  serialize. Fable judges it **dominated by early lock release**; its
  remaining advantage is Q, which only G7.0's arm C prices. **Commission it
  only on measured evidence** (ruling 7).
- **Larger or fuller batches.** There are only 32 clients. Under G7, N
  self-sizes to rate × I; under the serial schedule N=32 idles the writer
  during H. The fill-only forecast was 1.9–2.1k (A) and 1.5–1.7k (B). A
  coalescing wait adds to Q; fill helps under G7 only as phase-locking.
- **`commit_delay`, global `synchronous_commit`, fsync.** `commit_delay`
  merges only concurrent committers' flushes, and the synchronous chain has
  none. Turning `synchronous_commit` off globally, or fsync, is forbidden;
  arm E differs in kind (§3.8). `wal_sync_method` or storage tuning is a
  matched-configuration change to every system, so it is for the author.
- **Same-connection protocol pipelining** of k+1's P1 behind k's COMMIT
  saves an R, but the backend runs P1 only after k's flush; the two-slot
  design subsumes it. **Two-phase commit** costs two flushes per batch and
  `max_prepared_transactions`. Rejected.
- **Head CAS without a ticket or without a nonce**: the race of §3.4 and
  the ABA case of §4.2. **No `_head` row**, head as `max(_commits.seq)`:
  saves one HOT update but changes every reader's head statement and the
  G5/G6 stamps, at unmeasured cost. Both rejected.
- **Pre-execution on readers with validation (K3/K4, "G7c").** Ingress-
  fetched facts validated against a footprint window remove P1 from X, but
  each request still pays two R and a sync, so it gains 1–2 ms of L and
  does not move A past its floor. It needs read-set windows and a
  fresh-authority amendment (round-trip §7), behind measured G7.3.

### 8.2 Witnesses

1. **Randomized serial-equivalence differential against N=1**, extending
   G3b's paired oracle (seeds, 192 members, both stores): random batch
   boundaries, d ∈ {1, 2, 3}, adversarial slot-reply order, hot keys and
   keyed duplicates across batches, refusals and barriers, comparing
   outcomes, meters, receipts, rows, sequences, change evidence, log images,
   outbox, head, revision and attempt. **Held-chain mode** holds k's final
   message until k+1 has interpreted, so every batch folds a non-empty
   chain (on SQLite, §6). Under injected failures the committer §3.5 replay
   oracle checks the committed history.
2. **Fault matrix between k and k+1**, with k+1 dispatched and not: apply
   failure in k; CAS mismatch; k terminated before and after COMMIT arrives;
   socket drop after dispatch; ticket timeout (k held by a sleeping deferred
   trigger); adoption between P1 and final; takeover and SIGKILL with both
   in flight. Each asserts §4.1, no reply before rerun, a contiguous head
   and exact counters.
3. **Drain and ABA.** (a) k unknown while k+1 is parked before its CAS: the
   barrier must not read `_head` before the T_{k+1} probe succeeds. (b) k
   known-aborts and its survivors are forced to leave k's numeric end values
   while k+1 waits on T_k: k+1 must fail on the attempt, and no rerun may
   dispatch before k+1 resolves. (c) Two and three consecutive unknowns.
   (d) A probe timeout falls to fenced termination.
4. **Provisional-state rollback.** After every known-abort and unknown case,
   each §3.7 row's digest equals the serial reference's after the last
   released batch.
5. **Transaction status.** After every fault case, `pg_xact_status` of each
   P1 xid matches its classification: confirmed ⇒ committed; known abort
   (including 55P03 and raised assertions, proving COMMIT was skipped) ⇒
   aborted; unknown ⇒ either.
6. **Visibility hammer.** In every concurrent REPEATABLE READ snapshot,
   `_head.seq` is the maximum contiguous `_commits.seq` and a batch's marker
   images exist if and only if its commits do; `/changes` pages are
   contiguous. G6 hits racing dispatch, confirmation and install never serve
   outside confirmed in-order publication, `read()` observes every closure,
   and unknown closes the gate.
7. **Locks.** Zero `pg_stat_database.deadlocks` delta on one store; waits
   only on tickets, never `_head` or disjoint `r` rows; a forced cross-store
   collision yields at most a 40P01 known abort.
8. **RT0 ratchets.** 3 (G7.2) or 2 (G7.3, E1) flights per batch for every
   N; tickets and markers add none; idle N=1 budgets unchanged.
9. **Release order** equals revision order under every completion order.
10. **E only.** An immediate PostgreSQL shutdown under load recovers a
    prefix containing every acknowledged write; instrumented readers never
    emit output above D; a lost marker reply is covered only by a later
    marker on a continuously open connection.

## 9. Amendments this RFC would make

- **Committer §3.4 and §7.** Pipelined batches use the ticket chain and the
  final message's nonce CAS; barriers keep §3.4 but write a fresh attempt.
  Adaptive sizing counts ticket waits.
- **Controls §3.3, §4.1, §6.** "Under the head lock" becomes "under the
  commit ticket" for pipelined batches; G6 closure becomes §3.6's count;
  under E, G5 linearizes by the D hold; §6's G7 row points here.
- **Round-trip §3.5.** Generation and receipt re-checks move after the
  ticket wait; receipt copies resolve through the fold, `_ops` as backstop.
  "COMMIT is its own flight" holds through G7.2; G7.3 adopts §10.7's.
- **Store-port §5.2, §7.2, §8.** Cascade rows and the drain rule; `_head`

  slots on their own threads, readers 31−d.

  early lock release with acknowledged durability from a relaxed
  `synchronous_commit=off` cell.

## 10. Rulings

The orchestrator decided rulings 1–6 and 7's disposition after the round-1
panel; ruling 8 is open for the author.

1. **Connection budget: yes.** The second writer comes from the 30 readers,
   keeping the matched 32; W2/A (11,313) and W1/B (8,000) are re-measured
   with 29 readers, 28 at d=3 (§8.3).
2. **Cascade reruns: yes.** One whole-batch fresh-fact rerun of innocent
   successors, in original order, after complete drainage, with original
   deadlines, as their one fallback (§4.2).
3. **Successor witness: no, for now.** Successor evidence reconciles
   internal state but neither resolves k's callers nor permits a §3.1 rerun
   (§4.3). Revisit once the nonce CAS has shipped and §8.2 items 3 and 5
   show its evidence is unambiguous. **Dissent (grok):** flip it now. A
   CAS-commit of k+1 resolves k as committed, a CAS failure observing k's
   start resolves it as a known abort that may rerun, and any other head
   stays sticky-unknown; under "no", an unkeyed member's durable write
   leaves its caller unknown.
4. **Two-flight final (G7.3): yes, conditionally** on controls §10.7's M2
   witnesses, server-side raising assertions, transaction-status witnesses
   and a ticket-wait timeout that provably skips COMMIT (§3.2, §8.2 item 5).
   The ticket wait widens the unknown window by up to one predecessor flush.
5. **G6 hit wait: yes.** A short bound, then G5, the bound measured on mixed
   workloads rather than fixed at two COMMIT p50s.
6. **Writer driver: yes.** Per-slot synchronous threads, off PIPE (§3.9).
7. **W4/A: undecided.** G7.0 measures arms S (ordered, synchronous), E
   (early lock release) and C (concurrent disjoint) with Snapback's real
   tail before any visibility-layer project. The options then: accept the
   loss and pursue B; commission C's publication protocol (§7); a matched
   sync-latency change for every system; or Q phase-locking in M3.
8. **Early lock release: open.** Is arm E (§3.8) admissible as a headline
   S1 cell, given that S1 method §4 fixes `synchronous_commit`, and may every
   control's advocate use it? E1 is built, and E replaces G7.4, only on yes.

G7.0 answers without a ruling: the K/H split; whether N=1 joins a
non-empty pipeline; and whether E's markers and C's commits merge flushes in
practice, with arm C's L once the fence and tail are on each transaction.
