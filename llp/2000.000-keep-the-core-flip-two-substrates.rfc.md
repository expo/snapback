## 1. Summary

LLP 2000 is right that the whole application is the unit of design, right
that ownership comes before abstraction, and right to ask for deterministic
simulation and an early real deployment. Its weakness is that it treats the
two structural choices that produced Snapback 2's costs as open research
questions, and then proposes a first experiment at exactly the scale where
those costs are invisible. This document takes a position on both, names
what to keep from Snapback 2 verbatim, and rewrites the first slice so it
can fail for the right reasons.

The position, in one paragraph. Snapback 2's *core* is excellent and should
be copied: the rule language and its uniformity analysis, the structural-fact
family, one data language, typed states with no silence, refusals that paste
a rewrite, personas, the successor classifier, the derived per-recipient
log, the loop verbs and their sub-second budgets. Two *substrates* under
that core are where the record shows the hours went, and both should be
flipped rather than re-investigated: (1) the static certificate should keep
certifying **authorization and shape** and stop pricing **bytes and steps**,
which move to a deterministic runtime meter with honest `capped`/abort
outcomes; and (2) the client should hold **a replica of the viewer's declared
partition** — the local-first shape every shipped sync engine and Snapback
2's own `deliver` log converge on — instead of a per-query result cache
proven by receipts. Those two flips dissolve most of the machinery that
leaked into the application (receipts, coverage, halos, validators, grants,
ID slices, drains, `withHistory`, follower inboxes, per-table retention
words) and turn the 28-second acquisition into a bulk stream whose floor is
the device's write speed. The first slice should be built on the **hub
fixture** (1,000 memberships, 500 follows, 100,000 messages), with the
day-two change designed before the day-one screen, a native client in the
first slice, and a deterministic simulator as the primary test harness.

Three claims this document makes that LLP 2000 does not:

1. **The costs have two named causes, and both are decisions, not
   mysteries.** Byte-priced static certification and the receipt-proven
   result cache account for every ergonomic residual in LLP 1018.008.009
   except documentation. §2 shows the mapping.
2. **The first experiment must include the hub.** Snapback 2 was "elegant on
   day one and hostile on day two" (LLP 1020) and broke at 1,000 memberships
   (LLPs 1023, 1024, 1025). A two-principal slice will pass and teach nothing.
3. **The process is part of the product.** Seventy-one LLPs, over two
   thousand commits, and seven ceiling raises in fourteen days produced a
   sound system nobody can hold in their head. Snapback 3 needs one owner on
   the core, a simulator instead of a test matrix, and budgets anchored to
   competitors so that raising one means admitting a loss.

Against that, what scored: on the 2026-09-05 three-scorer Clique run
Snapback 2 beat Convex on authorization (82 vs 79) and offline (81 vs 44)
and lost on queries and live (60 vs 85), client integration (59 vs 79),
setup and docs (55 vs 79), diagnostics and loop (66 vs 77), operations (53
vs 76) and scale (62 vs 80); overall 66 vs 81. The later single-lane 0.0.21
and 0.0.25 scores of 82 and 85 are real improvement and not a three-scorer
comparison. The wins are the core; the losses are composition, discovery,
scale and operations — the substrates and what sits on them.

LLP 2000 §2 summarises this as "costs arise where one layer must reconstruct
another layer's facts." True, and the layer doing the reconstructing is the
client, because it was designed as a cache that may not compute. Name the
cause and the remedy follows.

## 3. Where LLP 2000 is right, and should not be diluted

- **The whole application is the unit.** §3's four properties (composition,
  locality, continuity, performance) are the right acceptance vocabulary.
  Keep them as the headings of the scenario suite.
- **Ownership before abstraction.** §5's table is the best paragraph in the
  document. §8 below turns its fourth row into a fixed vocabulary.
- **Composed reads must not fabricate.** Unknown totals stay unknown; revoked
  rows do not linger. Both flips below preserve this; they change what is
  *known*, not what is *claimed*.
- **Writes have one lifecycle with durable admission distinct from commit
  and stable operation identity.** Keep LLP 1018.004's three outcomes.
- **Rust and SQLite.** Yes, and the engine's transaction, interpreter, rule
  evaluation and successor logic are the pieces to copy first (§5).
- **Prototype the experience before the core.** Yes — and §8 makes the
  prototype's API signatures binding on the implementation, not the reverse.
- **Deterministic simulation early**, citing TigerBeetle. This is the single
  most important sentence in LLP 2000 and §11 makes it the primary harness.
- **A real deployment and a native client early.** Yes; §11 puts the native
  client in slice one.
- **Parallel, fenced, 2000-series, promotion is the author's later call.** Yes.

## 4. Where LLP 2000 is wrong or leaves the wrong things open

### 4.1 It reopens the language and leaves the bound model open; invert that

§6 asks to "evaluate Quarry" and "evaluate any alternative against the same
contracts." The one-language decision took thirty-seven paper applications,
a three-family panel, a spelling spike and an author ruling (LLP
0329.014.004 §9, LLP 1004, LLP 1007 §4b) to close, and the shipped result is
the most readable artifact in Snapback 2: the chat schema is 129 lines and a
query is three to six. Reopening the language will consume the first month.
What *should* be reopened is the bound model under it, which §6 phrases as a
comparison to "concentrate early investigation on." Half of the Quarry
refusals a builder meets are the certifier's byte and step prices wearing
Quarry syntax; the `fn`/`proc` split and the page-to-list gap (LLP 1018.008
F4) are bounded-composition restrictions the pricing model forced. Decide
the bound model (§6), keep the language, and re-derive its restrictions.

### 4.2 The first slice is at the scale where Snapback 2 looked elegant

§10 proposes "a private conversation with paginated messages and an unread
count," "two principals and two clients." Snapback 2 passed that slice in
its first week. It broke at the hub: 1,000 memberships refused the digest
fold, made global search cost a thousand requests, and refused the
envelopes builders first wrote (LLPs 1023, 1024, 1025). Every one of those
was discovered by a cold builder on a published package weeks after the
architecture was fixed. The first slice must include the hub principal from
day one (§11).

### 4.3 It investigates the client data model; the field has answered

§6 says "test a canonical local store with deliberate derived indexes,
durable pending operations, and explicit coverage and authorization
knowledge." That sentence describes a replica; the field has shipped it
under several names (buckets, mutators and a change stream, per-device
queues), Snapback 2's own LLP 1012 §6 reaches it by the hard road ("the
coverage rule that makes everything on the phone exact" is a fully drained
partition), and LLP 1019's `deliver` already computes the audience the
engine must maintain. Decide it (§7) and spend the investigation budget on
the two things the field has *not* settled for this design: partition
inversion of Quarry rules, and horizons at hub scale.

### 4.4 "Builder, human or agent" is two audiences

§3's first row conflates them. Agents need structured refusals with a
pasted rewrite, a one-screen grammar, and a served guide keyed by symbol.
Humans need a language server, a formatter, a dev overlay that shows the
live data and the queries watching it, and error text that reads as a
sentence. Snapback 2 measured only the agent (correction loops,
time-to-green) and shipped no editor support. "Beautiful" for the human
builder is mostly tooling, and it is cheap if it is in slice one and
expensive if it is retrofitted onto a parser that was never an LSP.

### 4.5 It inherits Snapback 2's process shape

§9 and §11 are careful about *release* apparatus and silent about *design*
apparatus. The Snapback 2 record is seventy-one LLPs numbered 1000 and up
in fourteen days, over two thousand commits on `main`, four-to-six-model
panels per RFC, three fix rounds per lane, and a runtime ceiling raised
seven times under a rule that says "a feature that does not fit is descoped
or paid for by deletion." The rigor was right for authorization and custody
and is why the core is sound; the cycle from "a builder felt friction" to
"the friction is gone" is a week and adds mechanism each time. §12 proposes
a smaller shape.

## 6. Flip 1: certify authorization statically, meter resources at runtime

**What stays static.** Every read site's table, index, prefix template,
order and `take`; whether the read rule is provably uniform over the
scanned prefix (`decided_true`, hoisting); the completeness class
(`complete | capped | maintained`); the structural-fact refusals (a count,
a page length, a `hasMore`, a loop tally over a non-uniform scan is not a
fact about the resource); disclosures; which mutations are locally
predictable (§7.3). All of this is authorization and semantics. It is what
makes a Snapback program safe to admit to every client, and it is what
Convex, Supabase and PocketBase do not have.

**What becomes dynamic.** Rows examined, index entries touched, rule probes,
interpreter steps, transaction heap, output bytes and rows written are
charged by a deterministic meter as the program runs, on the server and in
the client interpreter alike. The ceilings keep their names and roughly
their values. Exceeding one has a typed outcome per kind:

- A **query** returns `capped` at the meter with no continuation past the
  cut, exactly as LLP 1001 §3's "empty capped page is the terminal drain
  state" already behaves for a hidden run. Reads never abort.
- A **mutation** aborts and rolls back with `E_BOUND` in family `bound`,
  naming the *actual* meter, the actual row and site, and the same rewrite
  families Snapback 2 pastes today (a smaller page, a maintained aggregate,
  a `chunk` in a job).
- A **job** is chunked and resumable by construction (LLP 1016) and reports
  measured cost per chunk.

**What `check` and `test` say instead of refusing.** `check --cost` prints
the *structural* cost class from the static shape — constant, one page,
lanes × page, fan-out × page — with the sites that produce it. `test` runs
every operation against the scale fixture (§11) and prints *measured*
meters beside the ceilings. A builder learns that a query examines 5,113
rows on the hub principal by reading a number, not by being refused on a
bound that assumed the widest row 244 times.

**Why this is sound.** The Snapback 2 corpus already contains the argument.
"A denied candidate consumes the certified examined and rule-probe budgets,
never `take`"; a hoisted-false range "halts after the single determination
without fetching or charging the hidden population row by row"; a
non-uniform scan cut by the budget returns "the visible rows found so far
and the opaque `Capped` label" (LLP 1001 §3). A runtime meter applies the
same policy at the same points, so the hidden-population oracle is
identical to today's: none where the rule hoists, and an opaque cap where
it does not. Padded execution for secrecy-sensitive programs (LLP
0329.014.004 §2.2 L) remains a declared program property enforced by the
meter reporting the bound instead of the actual.

**What it dissolves.** Every row of §2 marked "static pricing"; the loop-
carried-value pricing problem (LLP 1020); the `fn`/`proc` split and the
page-to-list gap, which existed to keep collections priceable; seven
diagnostic subfamilies about byte products; the `certificate/search/closure.rs`
and `cap.rs` estimators; the argument about whether 4 MiB is the right
closure ceiling (there is no closure).

**What it costs.** A program can ship that is fine on the seed and expensive
on the hub, and the failure surfaces at runtime for that user. The
mitigations are the scale fixture in `test`, the structural cost class in
`check`, and the meter as backstop — which is the posture of every backend
Snapback is compared against, and those backends score 80–85 on queries.
The DoS argument is unchanged: no request does more than the meter allows.

## 7. Flip 2: the viewer's partition is the unit of sync

### 7.1 The declaration

A table is either **synced** — the engine maintains, per principal, the set
of its rows that principal may read, as a change log — or it is
**online-only**. The word goes on the table, where LLP 0329 said capability
must be legible:

```q
table messages:
  conversationId: conversations
  authorId: principal
  body: text <=4000 ?
  at: time
  by byConvTime: conversationId, at, id
  read <- exists memberships[.conversationId, viewer]
  sync to memberships[.conversationId] last 500 by byConvTime
```

`sync to memberships[.conversationId]` is LLP 1019's `deliver to
memberships[.conversationId]` generalized: the engine inverts the read rule
into an audience it can maintain (a membership row's insert adds a
conversation's rows to a principal's partition by `bootstrap`; its delete
emits one scope tombstone). `last 500 by byConvTime` is the **horizon**: the
partition holds at most that many rows per group, and older rows are
online-only. A table whose rule the engine cannot invert has no `sync`
line and is online-only; `check` says which rule term prevented it and
pastes the shape that would (an equality prefix on a principal or a key).
A `maintain` whose group key is in a synced table is delivered into the
partition as a value row, so a count is a local fact and the composite of
§2 row 6 needs no fallback.

### 7.2 What the client does with it

The client holds, per principal, one SQLite-shaped store of synced rows,
indexed as the schema declares; a per-partition **watermark** (one sequence
number); a durable **outbox** of mutations with client-minted ids; and the
interpreter. A query is a Quarry program run locally over the store. It is
`complete` inside the horizon and `partial` beyond it or at an online-only
site — the same honest states as today, with one reason each. A mounted
query is reactive: every applied batch re-runs the queries whose read sites
overlap the batch's tables (the trace-indexed subscription logic from
`live.rs`, moved to the client). A mutation runs locally first if it is
predictable (§7.3), producing `pending` rows with the ids it minted; then
it is sent; the server's commit arrives in the partition stream and
replaces the prediction.

Acquisition is the partition stream: one request per partition from the
watermark, the response chunked at a fixed byte size, each chunk applied in
one store transaction, the watermark advanced with it. There is no per-page
query, no receipt, no acknowledgement mutation. Delivery receipts (`with
receipts`) become one acknowledgement per stream position, which LLP 1019
already derives. Multi-tab is an SDK concern: one tab owns the store under
a Web Lock and the others read it through a shared worker; no application
sees `forwarding`.

Revocation: a scope tombstone removes the group's rows from the store, or
keeps them under the table's leave policy. LLP 1012 §5's two words survive
with their exact product meaning — `until-revoked` (the default: rows go)
and `delivered-history` (an expelled member keeps what was delivered, and
gets nothing new) — enforced by the client on the tombstone, not by a
receipt rule. A body deletion is an ordinary row tombstone. Neither needs
hidden-versus-absent reasoning on the client, because the client never
held a row it could not read.

### 7.3 Prediction, ids, generations

A mutation is **predictable** when every read it performs is over synced
tables and its writes touch synced tables; this is decidable at `check`
from the read-site shape and visible at the authoring site. A read outside
the partition (a cap over rows the viewer cannot see, an online-only table)
makes the mutation `pending` without a local row, never a fabricated one.
Prediction validity is settled at commit by the server, exactly as today.

Ids are **client-minted** (a time-ordered 128-bit form) for client inserts
and engine-minted for server writes; `new` on the client mints locally.
This deletes grants and slices. The cost is sixteen bytes per id and no
dense sequence, which nothing in the reference applications needs.

A partition stream is bound to a **schema generation**. An additive
successor applies in place; a narrowing successor re-bootstraps the
affected tables; a queued write carries the generation it was authored
under and is judged by LLP 1020's widening rules at the server. There is no
evidence identity to scope (§2 last row), because no held answer needs
re-authorizing — the partition is re-derived by the engine.

### 7.4 What it costs, honestly

- **Device storage is the partition, not what was looked at.** Bounded by
  horizons; this is what a local-first user expects and what the hub
  fixture must size. The hub principal's 1,000 conversations at `last 500`
  is an upper bound of 500,000 rows, so the horizon will be time- or
  count-based per app; the first slice measures it.
- **Server write amplification** for partition maintenance is real and is
  the price LLP 1019 already pays for `deliver`. Joining a large group is a
  bootstrap stream, not a write; leaving is one tombstone.
- **Online-only tables are a visible boundary.** A query mixing synced and
  online-only tables is `partial` offline at the online-only site. This is
  the honest form of Snapback 1's retention maze (LLP 0329 §"The
  mechanism") with the answer written on the table.
- **Two delivery modes, one language.** A client without a local store (an
  anonymous public feed, a dashboard) subscribes to server-evaluated live
  queries exactly as Snapback 2 does today. A client with a store subscribes
  to its partitions and evaluates locally. Same programs, same interpreter,
  different transport, chosen by the app's client configuration.
- **The security argument changes shape and keeps its strength.** Snapback
  2: "the client never computes a fact the receipt did not cover." Snapback
  3: "the client only holds rows in its partition, and the partition is its
  read authority, computed by the engine." Same guarantee, one mechanism.

### 7.5 What it dissolves

Every row of §2 marked "result cache." Concretely: `withHistory`,
`defineItems`, `mutationTarget`, `drain`, grants, ID slices, follower
inbox rows, receipts, coverage, extents, halos, validators, evidence
identity, and the fourteen twin inputs that are not the interpreter. The
global search of LLP 1024 becomes one local query over the partition's
word-prefix index within the horizon plus one server query beyond it. The
digest of LLP 1025 is a server job over server data, which flip 1 lets run.

## 8. The client vocabulary, fixed before the mechanism

LLP 2000 §4's experience prototype should produce, and §11's evaluation
should hold the implementation to, the complete set of states a screen can
show. It fits on an index card, and the mechanism must fit the card:

```ts
// A read. `complete` is false beyond the horizon or at an online-only site.
type Read<T> = { data: T; complete: boolean; fresh: boolean; since?: number; next?: Cursor }
            | { loading: true } | { denied: Refusal };
// A row this device changed and the server has not yet confirmed.
row.pending: boolean;
// A write. Three outcomes, all terminal or durable (LLP 1018.004).
type Write = { state: "pending" } | { state: "sent"; seq: number } | { state: "failed"; why: Refusal };
// The app.
type Link = "online" | "offline" | "signed-out";
```

Everything Snapback 2's `Snapshot` carries beyond this is an answer to
`why`, and `why` is where it belongs. The test is not "can the mechanism
express its facts" but "does a screen need any word not on the card." The
chat `Thread` should be `useQuery` plus JSX; the `Composer` should be
`useMutation` plus a form; `shared/thread.ts` and `shared/status.ts`
should not exist.

## 9. One interpreter, three hosts — re-measure with today's numbers

Snapback 2 ruled "never a native module" and "no WASM anywhere" on a
measurement: a 7.8 KB TypeScript twin against a 109 KB read-only Rust/WASM
subset (LLP 0329.014.004 §7). The twin now measures 89,843 gzip bytes and
the public client 142,989, so the ruling's premise is gone, and LLP
0329.014.004 §2.2 P already concedes a device-plane runtime for
device-authoritative partitions.

Under flip 2 the twin is the interpreter, the value model, the index and a
store adapter — roughly what the spike measured. So the recommendation is
not "switch to WASM"; it is: **rebuild the twin over the replica, measure
it, and set its budget from a competitor's shipped client** (§12). If it
lands near 20 KB, TypeScript stays on web. On Expo, SQLite is already
native (the carrier is `expo-sqlite`), so the Rust core as a native module
is the likely winner there and removes the two-interpreter conformance
obligation on the platform where process death and media make it hardest
to hold. Web keeps the corpus as its contract. Two client **tiers** — an
online-only client with no store and no interpreter, and a local-first
client with both — are one language and one server, and the tier is chosen
in the app's client configuration.

## 10. Effects: measure the sandbox against the builder's Node

Snapback 2 embeds Ibex 2 over Hermes for effects: a 7.9 MB binary against
4.5 MB without, a subset standard library, no imported helpers, "Node/npm
SDK compatibility is explicitly not promised," and a steady stream of
tickets (dropped fetch headers, string subset, `Intl` declarations exceeding
the runtime). LLP 2000 §6 asks to "test ordinary imported helpers and
representative libraries before settling the effects runtime" and says "a
custom environment must repay those costs." Agreed, with the prior stated:
the reference applications need a push notification, an email, a webhook
and an image resize, and every one of those is easiest in the builder's own
Node with an npm SDK. The outbox contract — durable job identity,
idempotency key, at-least-once, `sent_unknown` — is enforced by the
**server** and does not depend on the sandbox. Grants (allowed origins) can
be enforced by routing the worker's `fetch` through the owner. What an
embedded engine buys is tenant isolation and deterministic replay; the
former belongs to the hosted target's process model and the latter to the
outbox. The strategic weight of Ibex is the author's to place; this
document only records that the record's effect friction is the subset, not
the model.

## 11. The first slice, revised

LLP 2000 §10's stages are right. Their content changes in five ways.

1. **The fixture is the hub.** The Clique generator's `u0001` (100 groups,
   900 DMs, 1,000 memberships), `u0500` (500 follows), and 100,000 messages
   are the cold-acquisition dataset from day one, not a later scale step.
   "Search everything," "unread across everything," and the period digest
   are in the first scenario set, before media.
2. **Day two before day one.** Before the first screen: what identifies a
   generation; what a queued write is bound to; what a client holding a
   partition does on an additive, a widening, a narrowing and a rule change;
   what a rename does. The scenario suite gains "change a rule while a
   write is queued" and "rename a column with an old client attached."
3. **Native in slice one.** The Expo client's physics (no streaming
   `Response` body, `AVPlayer` refusing data URLs, process death mid-write)
   decide §9, so they cannot wait for slice three.
4. **Floors before targets.** Before building the sync path, measure and
   record: one gzipped response of 10,000 rows; one IndexedDB transaction
   writing 10,000 rows; one `expo-sqlite` bulk insert of 10,000 rows; a
   local query over 100,000 rows in the TypeScript interpreter and in
   SQLite; a 50-member send's partition fan-out on the server. The design
   target is within 2× of the floor. Snapback 2 spent two weeks moving 67 s
   to 28 s on a design whose floor was a second.
5. **The simulator is the harness.** One deterministic program under a
   seed: a single-threaded scheduler, a virtual clock, in-process server and
   N clients, and fault injection — drop, reorder, duplicate and delay
   messages; fail `fsync`; kill a process at any await; skew a clock. Quarry
   programs are already deterministic, so any failure replays from its
   seed. The invariants it checks on every commit: no silent state; a row
   revoked at sequence S never renders after S; a write with a given id
   applies at most once; every prediction converges to the server's state
   or a typed conflict; every screen state is on the card; watermarks are
   monotone; after faults stop, every client converges. Real-browser and
   device witnesses cover only what the simulator cannot: IndexedDB and Web
   Lock semantics, iOS process death, the React Native stream body, media
   playback. Snapback 2 carries 38,815 lines of tests; the simulator plus a
   dozen witnesses should replace most of them.

The slice's observable outcomes in LLP 2000 §10 stand, with two additions:
(6) the hub principal's first useful inbox renders within the §7 budget
while acquisition runs, and global search returns within one round trip;
(7) the loop bar — a fresh agent, the published package, reduced Clique
M1–M3, under 1,200 s with at most one Snapback-attributed correction — is
run in the slice's first week and weekly after, on the same harness and
scorers as Snapback 2, with Convex as the control. Snapback 2 never met it
at 0.0.21 and has not re-measured it in five releases.

## 12. Building Snapback 3 without rebuilding Snapback 2's process

- **One owner on the core for slice one.** LLP 2000 §8 asks for one person
  responsible for coherence. Extend it: one session at a time changes the
  engine, the store and the client contract until the slice runs; fan out
  only for measurements, reviews and the reference apps. Concurrency was
  Snapback 2's velocity and its incoherence.
- **Budgets anchored to competitors, held by deletion.** The online-tier
  client's gzip size is measured against Convex's shipped React client; the
  local-first tier's against PowerSync's web client; the server binary
  against PocketBase. Raising a budget then means writing "we are now
  larger than X," which is harder to do quietly than moving 85,000 to
  90,000. The loop budgets stay Snapback 2's: `check` under a second,
  `doctor` under half, the blocking suite under a minute.
- **No ceiling is raised in slice one.** If a feature does not fit, the
  design changes or the feature waits. This rule already exists in
  `rules/NOT-DOING.md`; Snapback 3 should be the first place it holds.
- **One design document, updated in place; one experiment journal,
  append-only.** LLP 2000 says this in §9. The journal replaces the Revised
  header as a change log. Panels are for authorization and custody
  mechanisms; everything else is judged by the simulator, the scenario
  suite and the weekly Clique run.
- **The scenario suite is the spec.** LLP 2000 §4's six scenarios,
  executable, on the hub fixture, in the simulator, are what "done" means.
  A document with no scenario behind it is a proposal (which is
  `rules/RULES.md`'s rule already).
- **The human builder is measured too.** Time for a person to add a field
  and see it in the editor, the overlay and the running app; whether the
  language server catches the refusal before `check` does; whether a
  screen's states are explained in a sentence.

## 13. Risks, and what would falsify this

| Claim | Falsified if |
| --- | --- |
| Partition inversion covers the reference apps | The messenger or the field-work app needs a synced table whose read rule the engine cannot invert and cannot be restructured to a key prefix without changing the product |
| The horizon keeps the hub's partition affordable | The hub principal's partition at a product-honest horizon exceeds the device budget or the acquisition budget by more than 2× the measured floor |
| Runtime metering does not regress disclosure | The simulator finds an observable difference between a static ceiling cut and a metered cut on any non-uniform scan |
| The twin over a replica is small | It measures above the competitor-anchored budget after the store adapter and index are in, and WASM measures larger still |
| Client-minted ids are enough | A reference application needs a dense engine sequence for a client insert |
| The simulator replaces the matrix | A class of defect the real-browser witnesses catch repeatedly that the simulator's fault model cannot express |
| One owner is faster than the fleet | Slice one does not run the hub fixture within four weeks of the experience prototype |

If the first slice cannot reach the loop bar, cannot acquire the hub
fixture within 2× of its measured floor, or cannot hold the client under
its competitor-anchored budget, the thesis is wrong and Snapback 2 should
receive whichever pieces (the meter, the partition stream, the simulator)
proved out on their own.

## 14. Open questions for the author

1. **Is `sync` a per-table word, or does every table with an invertible
   rule sync by default with an explicit `online-only`?** Default-sync is
   the local-first posture; explicit is the legible one. Recommendation:
   explicit, because the horizon has to be chosen anyway.
2. **What is the default horizon?** A count per group (`last 500`), a time
   window (`last 30d`), or both? Recommendation: both allowed, count
   required.
3. **Does the online-only tier ship in slice one?** It is small and it is
   what an anonymous feed needs. Recommendation: yes, because it is the
   control for every "local-first made this slower" measurement.
4. **Rust core as a native module on Expo — decide now or after §9's
   measurement?** Recommendation: after, but with the measurement in slice
   one's first week.
5. **Effects host.** Builder's Node, embedded engine, hosted isolate — or
   all three behind one contract? Recommendation: measure the four
   reference effects on each; the strategic question about Ibex is yours.
6. **Which client-minted id form?** A time-ordered 128-bit form is the field
   default; the engine's namespace rule (LLP 1006 §9a) must accept it.
7. **Does LLP 2000 absorb this document, or stand beside it?** The parent's
   §6 and §10 would change; the rest would not.
