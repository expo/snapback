# LLP 0329.014.009: The Quarry authoring test — does a from-scratch notation change how models fail?

**Type:** Research
**Status:** Draft
**Systems:** Authoring surface, Developer experience, Agent experience, Code generation
**Author:** Charlie Cheever / Claude Fable 5 (orchestrator); authors under test: codex gpt-5.6-sol, grok-4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5
**Date:** 2026-08-31
**Revised:** 2026-08-31
**Related:** LLP 0329.014, LLP 0329.014.000, LLP 0329.014.004 (§9, the spelling
result), LLP 1000, `spikes/quarry-authoring/`,
`snapback2/packages/compiler/fixtures/first-attempts/`

## Summary

Spike S2 established that the TypeScript spelling's "looks like TypeScript but
isn't" hazard is *real but bounded*: eight realistic first attempts were all
refused with a compiling rewrite, and 66.7% of raw gauntlet code lowers
unchanged. This experiment asks the next question: how much of the
first-attempt defect rate is **prior collision** — the TS pretraining prior
suggesting constructs the certificate refuses — versus inherent task
difficulty? The instrument is **Quarry**, a paper notation designed so the
certificate is the grammar (bounds are mandatory syntax, page reductions are
`capped` types, the two clocks are incomparable types, writes are sigils; the
sketch design is recorded in the conversation that produced this LLP and in
`spikes/quarry-authoring/packet/quarry-spec.md`). Three model families
authored ten trap-seeded tasks in three arms — TS without the policy brief,
TS with it, Quarry with its spec — one shot, no compiler, sandboxed away from
this repository. TS arms are machine-graded through the real
lowering+checker pipeline; all arms are rubric-graded against reference
solutions (the TS references pass the checker).

The hypothesis (author, 2026-08-31): unfamiliarity prevents models from
stumbling into moves TS invites but the checker bans; the counter-hypothesis
is that it merely trades those defects for hallucinated syntax. The
asymmetry that matters: TS defects are *plausible* (they parse; one class
even certifies), Quarry defects should be *loud*. A defect that cannot parse
is cheap in an agent loop; a defect that certifies and is wrong is the most
expensive kind there is.

## 1. Protocol

- **Arms.** A: TS, API reference only — the "agent assumes it can" condition.
  B: TS, API reference + the checker's eight policy rules. C: Quarry, complete
  one-page spec. B and C are length-matched (8.3 KB vs 7.7 KB); A is B minus
  policy (5.7 KB). All arms carry the same two worked examples (thread, send)
  in their own notation. Briefs: `spikes/quarry-authoring/packet/`.
- **Tasks.** Ten (`packet/tasks.md`), each seeding one trap from the S2
  first-attempt taxonomy or acting as a control: T01 control join, T02
  unbounded scan, T03 structural count, T04 structural rank, T05 intent
  clock, T06 unbounded recursion, T07 fanout, T08 missing guard, T09 capped
  display count, T10 capacity race. T10 is the **admissible-but-wrong**
  probe: a bounded page-count capacity check passes the checker (verified
  against the real pipeline before the run) and overbooks at scale; only a
  maintained count is complete.
- **Authors.** codex gpt-5.6-sol (xhigh), grok-4.6 (xhigh), Claude Haiku 4.5 —
  three families; the small model is the most prior-dependent instrument. One
  fresh CLI invocation per (model × arm × task), cwd an empty sandbox with no
  repository access (the reference solutions live in-tree). 90 generations,
  one shot, no repair round.
- **Grading.** TS submissions run through
  `snapback2/packages/compiler` (`bun src/cli.ts <module> --synthetic-schema`)
  for a machine verdict (families `E_UNBOUND_SCAN`, `E_UNBOUND_COUNT`,
  `E_STRUCTURAL_COUNT`, `E_AMBIENT_TIME`, `E_OPAQUE`, …). All submissions are
  rubric-graded (`packet/references/rubric-and-references.md`) on per-task
  items in four buckets: **PRIOR** (prior-collision defect), **SEM**
  (admissible-but-wrong), **SYN** (syntax/API fumble — scored separately so
  notation noise cannot masquerade as the semantic signal), **INC**
  (incompleteness). Quarry has no checker; arm C is rubric-only, which the
  analysis must keep visible. A sample is cross-graded by a non-author family
  for agreement.

## 2. Results

All 90 generations completed; none failed or timed out. Full grading record:
`spikes/quarry-authoring/grading/` (machine verdicts, rubric notes, raw
modules, cross-grade).

### 2.1 Prior-collision defects (the primary outcome)

| Arm | codex | grok | haiku | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — TS, no policy brief | 0 | 0 | **3** | **3/30** |
| B — TS + policy brief | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0/30 |
| C — Quarry | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0/30 |

Haiku-A committed exactly the taxonomy's defects: an unbounded scan (T02), a
structural loop tally over a private table (T03), an unbounded fanout scan
(T07) — every one caught by the real checker (`E_UNBOUND_SCAN`,
`E_STRUCTURAL_COUNT`). The same model committed zero in arm B and zero in
arm C. The frontier models committed zero everywhere — including four traps
that never fired for anyone: the intent clock (T05: all nine used
`commitTime`/`commit` in the expiry guard), the missing guard (T08), ambient
time (nobody invented `Date`), and `while`/recursion (T06: everyone reached
for `recurse`/`walk`).

### 2.2 The TS subset's jagged edge (the surprise)

15 of 60 TS submissions were machine-refused, but only 3 for policy. The
other **12 are `E_OPAQUE` subset-strictness refusals of semantically correct
code**, hit by *every* model in arms A **and** B: block-bodied `map`
callbacks, `recurse` call shapes the lowering doesn't recognize, a missing
`fanout` option, `String(...)`, destructuring. T06 is the emblem: all six
frontier TS submissions (codex and grok, both arms) are semantically perfect
bounded traversals, and four of six were refused on `recurse` spelling
trivia. The policy brief fixed policy defects; it cannot fix the invisible
boundary of "which TypeScript am I allowed to write." (Honest caveat: some of
this is S1-lowering immaturity rather than anything intrinsic — a mature
front end would accept a block-bodied map callback. But the boundary's
*invisibility* is intrinsic to the subset-of-a-big-language approach: no
reference can enumerate what a model already believes about TypeScript.)

### 2.3 Quarry syntax under three unfamiliar model families

Zero hallucinated operators. Zero unbounded constructs (they have no
spelling; none were invented). Every range read carried its mandatory bound.
All ten C-arm maintained-aggregate declarations were well-formed, including
unprompted `(readable: public)` where disclosure was genuinely required
(T04). The failures that did occur are two *convergent* invented
ergonomics, produced independently by all three families at seven sites:
**auto-dereference through a ref binding** (`journalId.owner`,
`#seatCount[ride]`, `bookings[.]`, `walk e up`) and **bare-name shape
fields** (`{ title, startsAt }`). Convergent invention is a design verdict,
not noise: the language should adopt both (§3).

### 2.4 Admissible-but-wrong (T10) and the disclosure spelling

The pre-verified trap — a bounded page-count capacity check that *passes the
checker* and overbooks — was committed by **nobody** in any arm: all nine
submissions declared a maintained passenger count and guarded with it
(the "hundreds may try to join at once" cue plus a visible `maintained`
facility in both surfaces was evidently sufficient). Two frontier models
additionally invented a defensive `hasMore` refusal on the T07 fanout cap
unprompted. One asymmetry did show: only Quarry has a *spelling* for
disclosure — codex once over-disclosed (`readable: public` on an
employer-scoped count, T03), which is reviewable precisely because it is
written down; the TS arms had nowhere to say it at all.

### 2.5 Size economics

With schema stripped, mean program size: arm A 502 bytes, arm B 521 bytes,
arm C **198 bytes** — a per-(task, model) B/C ratio of **2.92× mean, 2.83×
median** (range 1.05–5.57, n = 30). The predicted 3–5× token claim holds at
the low end in characters, on real submissions rather than reference
solutions.

### 2.6 Cross-grade

A 12-submission sample (39 rubric items) spanning models, arms, and tasks
was independently graded by codex gpt-5.6-sol against the same rubric and
spec: **37/39 agreement (94.9%)**. Both disagreements are one axis — whether
an invented Quarry shorthand with correct semantics is a syntax fumble
(orchestrator) or a strict-invalid item failure (codex). The primary tallies
keep the semantics reading (an unparseable guard never admits an unguarded
write), and the disagreement is disclosed here because the strict reading
adds 2 haiku-C failures — both *loud*, parse-refused ones, which reinforces
the loud-vs-plausible asymmetry rather than weakening it. The cross-grader
also caught the packet's own spec gap: the given T03/T08 read rules access
fields through a `Row?` — more evidence for adopting auto-deref
(`grading/cross-grade/codex-verdicts.md`).

## 3. Reading the result

1. **The false-affordance effect is real, small-model-concentrated, and
   fully absorbed by either treatment.** Prior-collision defects appeared
   only where the prior was strongest relative to instruction-following
   (haiku) and only with no rules in context (arm A). Both the policy brief
   and the from-scratch notation eliminated them completely. For frontier
   models on these ten tasks, the false-affordance hazard was already ~zero
   with nothing but an API reference — S2's "real but bounded" verdict
   re-confirmed from a new angle, and bounded harder than expected.
2. **What the from-scratch notation actually buys is not fewer semantic
   errors — it is a *clean failure channel* plus the token economics.** TS's
   residual failure mode (12/60 refusals) is semantically-correct code
   falling off an invisible subset boundary; that churn class is
   *unrepresentable* in Quarry because the whole grammar is the boundary and
   it fits on a page. Add 2.9× smaller programs — compounding across every
   read, review, diff, and re-emission in an agent loop — and the notation's
   case rests on loop-cost, not correctness.
3. **The experiment worked as a language-design instrument, which may be its
   most useful role.** Three blind families converged on the same two
   ergonomic extensions; the spec gaps they exposed (shape-on-optional,
   arg/column shadowing in `[code = code]`) are v0.2 work items. Running
   unfamiliar models against a paper grammar is a cheap, repeatable design
   review.
4. **What this does not show:** one-shot, ten tasks, no repair loop — it
   measures first-attempt collision, not authoring-to-green cost (S2 showed
   TS converges in 1–2 refusal rounds; Quarry has no compiler to converge
   against). Arm C is rubric-graded while TS arms had a machine checker, an
   asymmetry that favors Quarry on anything the rubric can't see. The traps
   were *stated* in task prose (guards, caps, "hundreds at once") — this
   tests execution under salient requirements, not requirement recall. And
   n = 3 per cell for the primary effect.

## Confidence

High that the arm-A haiku defects and all machine verdicts are as recorded
(the real checker produced them; artifacts are in-tree). High on the subset-
strictness finding (12 refusals, every model, both TS arms). Medium-high on
"Quarry syntax held" (30 submissions, one grader, cross-grade sampled).
Medium on the generalization from 10 tasks and 3 models; low-medium on
anything about authoring *cost* (no repair loop was run). The size ratio is
solid for these tasks but characters are a proxy for tokens.

## Open questions

1. Does the effect invert with repair rounds — i.e., does TS's
   refuse-with-rewrite loop close the gap at lower total cost than Quarry's
   larger first-attempt reliability, once a Quarry checker exists?
2. Do harder, *unstated* traps (requirements the task prose does not
   telegraph) restore a frontier-model false-affordance effect?
3. Adopt auto-deref and bare-name shape fields into the Quarry sketch? (The
   evidence says yes; both are unambiguous given typed refs.)
4. Is the right production posture the one this experiment reinforces:
   TS authoring + Quarry as the display/trace form (LLP 0329.014.004 §9),
   with the subset's jagged edge sanded by teaching the lowering more
   spellings — or does the 12/60 E_OPAQUE rate argue the subset boundary is
   a permanent tax worth escaping?
