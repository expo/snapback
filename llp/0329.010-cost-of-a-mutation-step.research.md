# LLP 0329.010: The cost of adding a mutation step

**Type:** Research
**Status:** Draft
**Systems:** Engine, Storage, Code generation, Client, Sync, Testing
**Author:** Charlie Cheever / Claude

**Related:** LLP 0329, LLP 0329.005, LLP 0329.006, LLP 0329.007, LLP 0329.008,

`tests/conformance/data-tier/`

## Summary

[LLP 0329.007](./0329.007-is-the-point-read-restriction-deliberate.research.md)
established that a bounded read step is permitted but unbuilt;
[LLP 0329.008](./0329.008-coterie-plan-expressibility.research.md) established that
it plus two extensions closes all 25 of `coterie`'s mutations. The remaining
question for a decision is what it costs.

**Answer: roughly 15 files and ~50 sites across four layers, plus 4–5 shared
conformance cases — a well-trodden path with an existing parity gate, not a
redesign.**

The measurement also surfaced the mechanism behind a number LLP 0329.005 reported
without explaining: **every mutation step is implemented twice, and that dual
implementation is exactly why declarative plans get optimistic prediction and JS
handlers do not.**

## Findings

### H1 — Every step exists twice, and that is the feature

`MutationStep` is implemented in the Rust authority
(`crates/snapback-kernel/src/storage.rs`) **and** in a 714-line client evaluator
(`packages/snapback-client/ir-evaluator.mjs`). The client implements all eleven
generic and specialized variants:

```
AllocateSequence AssertCurrentRelation AssertSourceChanged Delete DeleteWhere
Get Guard Insert InsertEach Let Patch
```

A 728-line optimistic overlay (`packages/snapback-contract/derived-overlay.mjs`)
implements the generic eight:

```
Delete DeleteWhere Get Guard Insert InsertEach Let Patch
```

This reframes LLP 0329.005 F2's 19% optimistic-prediction ceiling. It is not an
arbitrary product restriction: **it is the reach of the client-side evaluator.** A
plan can be predicted locally because the client can re-execute its IR; a JS handler
cannot be shipped to the device, so it cannot. The capability difference LLP 0329.006
traced to the authoring form has its concrete mechanism here.

It also explains a cost that is easy to miss: a new step is not one implementation,
it is two that must agree.

### H2 — The measured change surface

Sites and files touched by existing step variants, across `crates/`, `packages/`,
and `schemas/`:

| Variant | Files | Sites |
|---|---|---|
| `AssertCurrentRelation` | 11 | 37 |
| `DeleteWhere` | 16 | 46 |
| `InsertEach` | 15 | 56 |
| `AllocateSequence` | 15 | 58 |

`DeleteWhere` is the closest analogue to a bounded read — it is the only existing
step that evaluates a filter over a table — and its 16 files decompose as:

- **Rust (5)** — `manifest/types.rs` (the enum), `value_expr.rs` (expression walk),
  `storage.rs` (execution), `engine.rs` (validation), plus tests;
- **Codegen (6)** — `define.mjs` (builder), `index.mjs`, `index.d.mts`,
  `write-policy.mjs` (LLP 0264 classification), `data-tier-corpus.mjs`, tests;
- **Client (4)** — `ir-evaluator.mjs`, `derived-overlay.mjs`, plus their tests;
- **Schemas (4)** — generated JSON schemas, regenerated from the Rust types per
  LLP 0037.

### H3 — The cross-language parity gate already exists

`tests/conformance/data-tier/` holds a committed corpus of **37 cases**, authored
through the real builder and serialized by the real codegen serializer
(`packages/snapback-codegen/data-tier-corpus.mjs`, LLP 0051 §D5). Its own comment
states the contract: "The committed JSON — not this module — is the cross-language
contract."

It is executed by the Rust runner (`crates/snapback/tests/data_tier_corpus_v0.rs`)
**and** by the client evaluator's test, which reads
`tests/conformance/data-tier/corpus.manifest.json` directly
(`packages/snapback-client/ir-evaluator.test.mjs:292`). So the two implementations
are gated against shared cases rather than trusted to agree.

Per-step corpus coverage:

| Step | Cases |
|---|---|
| `Patch` | 8 |
| `Insert` | 5 |
| `DeleteWhere` | 5 |
| `Get` | 5 |
| `Guard` | 4 |
| `Let` | 4 |
| `InsertEach` | 3 |
| `Delete` | 1 |

A new generic step would owe roughly 4–5 cases on this precedent.

### H4 — The specialized steps are outside both the corpus and the overlay

`AllocateSequence`, `AssertCurrentRelation`, and `AssertSourceChanged` have **zero**
corpus cases and are **absent from the optimistic overlay** (they appear in the
client evaluator but not in `derived-overlay.mjs`).

For `AllocateSequence` this is partly principled — a globally coordinated value
cannot be exactly predicted by a disconnected client, an information limit rather
than a gap. But the corpus absence has a more mundane cause: the corpus is authored
through `defineMutation`, and LLP 0329.006 F6 records that `allocateSequence` is
admitted only inside `defineWriteWithActivity`-generated plans. **A step gated out of
the hand-authored builder is therefore also gated out of the conformance corpus that
is the cross-language contract.** That is the LLP 0329.006 F6 pattern again, one
layer down.

### H5 — The visibility obligation is smaller than LLP 0329.007 estimated

LLP 0329.007 §"What would actually be new" named read-side visibility composition as
the one genuinely new obligation, on the grounds that `DeleteWhere` applies only an
owner-field write check. Tracing the executor shows the machinery is already present
and already invoked from a mutation step.

`crates/snapback-kernel/src/storage.rs`, continuing the `DeleteWhere` arm past the
`QueryPlan` construction quoted in LLP 0329.007 E4:

```rust
let (sql, params) = build_query_sql(
    table, &schema, &scan_plan, input, &bindings, direct_bindings.as_ref(),
    now_ms, Some(&next_id), &format!("{step_site}/delete-where"), … )?;
let rows = {
    let mut statement = tx.prepare(&sql)?;
    …
```

`build_query_sql` is the read path's own SQL builder, and it calls
`build_table_visibility_sql`, which handles **all four** `TableVisibilityV1` kinds —
`PublicRead`, `Owner`, `Membership { declaration, resource_field, roles }`, and
`AnyOf { arms }` — emitting a membership clause via `membership_clause` for the third.

So a mutation step today already builds a visibility-composed query plan, compiles it
through the shared read-path builder, executes it inside the transaction, and
**materializes rows**. `DeleteWhere` passes `Owner` only because an owner write field
is what a delete needs, and it deletes the rows it materialized rather than binding
them.

The remaining work for a bound read is therefore to pass the table's declared *read*
visibility instead of the owner write field, and to bind the rows instead of deleting
them. That is materially cheaper than LLP 0329.007's estimate.

**What does not become cheaper is the design question.** Which visibility a mutation's
bound read should compose — the caller's read policy, a stricter one, or a declared
one — and what the noninterference contract is when a refusal depends on rows the
caller cannot see, remain open and are not answered by the presence of the mechanism.
LLP 0120's noninterference finding (a count can disclose the existence of rows it does
not return) applies directly.

## Consequences

1. **The cost is bounded and the path is paved.** ~15 files, ~50 sites, ~4–5
   conformance cases, two implementations already gated by a shared corpus. This is
   meaningfully more than a one-file change and far less than a redesign.
2. **Budget for the client evaluator, not just the server.** Roughly a third of the
   change surface is client-side, and it is the part that produces the capability
   (prediction) the change exists to unlock.
3. **The 19% ceiling has a mechanism, not a policy.** Any argument about widening it
   is an argument about what the client evaluator can re-execute.
4. **Gating a step out of `defineMutation` also gates it out of the conformance
   contract.** If the bounded read ships behind a profile rather than in the
   hand-authored builder, it inherits `allocateSequence`'s fate: no corpus cases, no
   overlay support, and no demo uptake.

## Confidence

High on H1–H3: file and site counts are `grep` over the tree at `6cf939d31`, the
corpus count is parsed from its manifest, and the parity consumer is a cited line.

Medium on the cost *estimate* as a prediction. Counting the sites an existing
variant touches measures the shape of the change, not its difficulty. H5 removes the
part I had flagged as unpriceable — the visibility mechanism exists and is already
invoked from a mutation — but replaces it with a design question (which visibility to
compose, and the noninterference contract) that a site count cannot price either.

Medium-high on H4's causal reading. The corpus-absence-follows-builder-gating link is
inferred from the corpus module's own construction (it is authored with
`defineMutation`) rather than from a document stating the rule.

## Open questions

1. ~~What does the visibility composition cost specifically?~~ **Answered by H5: the
   mechanism exists.** The open part is the *policy*: which visibility should a
   mutation's bound read compose, and what is the noninterference contract when a
   refusal depends on rows the caller cannot see?
2. Should the bounded read enter through `defineMutation` directly rather than a new
   profile? H4 says the profile route costs it the conformance corpus and the
   optimistic overlay — the two things that would make it worth having.
3. Does `derived-overlay.mjs` need the new step to produce a prediction, or only
   `ir-evaluator.mjs`? The two have different step sets today, and which one gates
   rung C's capacity checks is not established here.
